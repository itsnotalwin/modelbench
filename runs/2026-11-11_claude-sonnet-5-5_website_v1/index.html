Gnomon

Every sundial is the same idea: a thin object that lets the sun write on the ground. Drag the light across this page and watch the shadow turn.

Set the latitude. Read the hour.

On a horizontal sundial the hour lines are not evenly spaced. Each sits at an angle θ from the noon line, where tan θ = sin φ × tan(15° × hours from noon) and φ is your latitude. Slide it and the whole face re-engraves itself.

The sun is not a clock.

Earth’s orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, so the true sun runs up to about sixteen minutes ahead of a steady clock in early November and fourteen behind in mid-February. Photograph the sun at the same clock time all year and it traces this figure eight.

Field notes

γνώμων

Greek for “one who knows”. On a sundial it is the blade or rod that casts the shadow. The rest of the instrument is a ruler for reading it.

Aim at the pole

To keep time all year, the gnomon’s edge must point at a celestial pole, so its tilt from the plate equals the latitude. That is why a sundial belongs to one place.

Older than clocks

Egyptian shadow clocks were in use by about 1500 BCE. Mechanical clocks arrived nearly three thousand years later and were corrected against sundials for centuries.

Everyone’s own noon

Until the railways, each town kept its own sun time. Standard time zones came in during the 1880s so that timetables could agree, which is why your clock and a sundial now part ways.
