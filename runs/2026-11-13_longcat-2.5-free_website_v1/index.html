Preface

Grey is a technology, not a colour.

White paper and black ink are two tones. Between them lies everything a newspaper ever printed: the face in the photograph, the sky behind it, the softness of a shadow. Halftone is the trick that bridges the gap — an illusion built from dots, sized by light, spaced by a screen.

This almanac takes the trick apart. Eight plates, eight working instruments: the screen, the frequency, the gain, the rosette, the dither, the grain, the type, the gloss. Every figure is computed in your browser, in a single bit, at the moment you meet it.

How to read this almanac

  • Each numbered plate is a live instrument, not a picture.
  • Drag a slider, move the pointer, or use the arrow keys.
  • Everything renders in one bit: on or off, black or white.

01

The Screen

A halftone screen trades tone for area. Each cell of the grid carries one dot; the darker the tone, the larger the dot. Drag the slider and watch one cell swell — the entire illusion is a simple exchange of size for shade.

Dot shape

One cell, magnified. The dot’s area — not its diameter — carries the tone.

02

Frequency

Screens are rated in lines per inch. A coarse screen breaks the image into visible dots; a fine screen hides them. The same relief is screened here through a loupe at ×8, so you can count the cells as the frequency climbs.

Newsprint sits near 85 LPI. A glossy magazine runs 150. Retina displays pretend the dots are not there at all.

03

Dot Gain

Ink spreads. On press, a dot almost always prints larger than it was drawn, and midtones swell the most. Compare the aim with the printed result, and read the tone-value increase as the gain climbs.

TVI peaks where the dot has the most neighbour to spread into — the midtones.

04

Rosettes

Two periodic screens superimposed will interfere. At a small angle difference the dots fall into a slow, large-scale beat — the moiré that ruins cheap scans, and the rosette that decorates four-colour print. Rotate the second screen and watch the pattern breathe.

The beat is the screen cell divided by twice the sine of half the angle. Small angles, large patterns.

05

Dithering

A halftone screen is one way to fake grey with one bit. Ordered dithering lays a threshold map over the image; error diffusion hands each pixel’s mistake to its neighbours. Both trade resolution for tone.

Algorithm

Ordered dithering keeps a fixed grid; error diffusion wanders. Both end in exactly two tones.

06

Grain

Amplitude-modulated screening varies the size of the dot; stochastic screening varies the number of dots. Drag the wipe to compare the two philosophies on the same field of rings.

AM dots touch at 50% and plug at 60%. FM dots never touch — the grain is in the spacing, not the spot.

08

Glossary

Sixteen terms, in the order a printer would meet them.

Amplitude-modulated screening: dots of varying size on a fixed grid. The amplitude of each dot carries the tone.

Frequency-modulated, or stochastic, screening: dots of fixed size at varying density. Also called stochastic screening.

Lines per inch: the frequency of a halftone screen. 85 LPI is newsprint; 150 LPI is a glossy magazine.

Dots per inch: the addressable resolution of an output device. A 1200 dpi platesetter can draw a very small dot.

The fraction of a cell covered by ink. A 50% dot covers half the cell; its diameter is about 71% of the cell.

The increase in dot area from film to print, caused by ink spreading into the paper. Also called tone value increase.

Tone value increase: the curve of aimed tone against printed tone. It peaks near 50%, where the dot has the most neighbour to spread into.

The interference pattern produced when two periodic screens overlap. A small angle difference yields a slow, large-scale beat.

The flower-like pattern formed by four process screens at conventional angles — 0°, 15°, 45°, 75°.

The orientation of a halftone screen, measured in degrees from horizontal. The key screen sits at 45°, where the eye is least sensitive to it.

An ordered-dither threshold map with a recursive structure. The 8×8 matrix disperses thresholds evenly across the cell.

A dithering method that pushes each pixel’s quantisation error to its neighbours, so the local average stays true.

The classic error-diffusion kernel: 7/16 right, 3/16 below-left, 5/16 below, 1/16 below-right.

A dither by Bill Atkinson that diffuses only six-eighths of the error, keeping more contrast in the highlights.

An image with exactly two tones: on and off. Every grey in this almanac is a trick played on the eye by one bit.

The smallest repeating unit of a halftone screen. It holds one dot, and its area is the screen’s atom.
