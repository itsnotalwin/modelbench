01 · System Overview

A Quiet Neighborhood

VEL-9 is a K-type main-sequence star, approximately 12.4 light-years from Sol in the direction of the constellation Eridanus. At 6.2 billion years old, it is older than our Sun by nearly two billion years — old enough that its planetary system has settled into a mature, dynamically quiet configuration.

The system hosts seven confirmed planets, designated VEL-9 b through h in order of discovery (not orbital distance). Three orbit within the conservative habitable zone: VEL-9 d, e, and f. A fourth, VEL-9 c, skims the inner edge with a runaway greenhouse atmosphere. The outer worlds, g and h, are ice giants in wide, near-circular orbits. The innermost, VEL-9 b, is a lava world tidally locked to its star.

"VEL-9 doesn't shout. It doesn't flare. It doesn't host hot Jupiters or disintegrating rocky cores. It simply persists — a metronome ticking in a galaxy full of noise." — Dr. Elara Voss, Lead Spectroscopist, VEL-9 Survey (Cycle 12)

What makes VEL-9 scientifically precious is precisely its ordinariness. Most exoplanet surveys are biased toward the loud and the extreme: transiting giants, ultra-short-period lava worlds, systems caught in violent migration. VEL-9 represents the quiet majority — the planetary systems that look, to our instruments, like slightly older, slightly calmer versions of home.

Stellar Parameters

Spectral Type

K2 V
Orange main-sequence star. Cooler, smaller, and longer-lived than the Sun. Peak emission shifted toward red/infrared.

Age

6.2 Gyr
Nearly 1.5× the Sun's age. Magnetic activity has largely ceased; stellar wind is weak and steady. Planetary atmospheres have had time to stabilize.

Metallicity

[Fe/H] = -0.12
Slightly metal-poor relative to Sol. Correlates with smaller, rockier planets and fewer gas giants — consistent with the observed architecture.

The Survey Mission

The VEL-9 Survey began in Cycle 1 (Earth year 2284) as a dedicated long-baseline astrometric and spectroscopic campaign. Unlike transit surveys, which require geometric alignment, the Survey uses precision radial velocity (PRV) combined with direct imaging coronagraphy from the Keppler-Station observatory at Sun-Earth L2.

This approach yields complete orbital solutions — true masses, not minimum masses — and enables atmospheric characterization of non-transiting worlds. The cost is time: a full orbital period for VEL-9 h is 84 Earth years. The Survey is a multi-generational project.

Observing Baseline

47 Cycles
Continuous PRV monitoring since Cycle 1. Direct imaging coronagraphy added Cycle 18. Astrometric confirmation from Gaia-Next Cycle 31 onward.

Precision

12 cm/s RV
Single-measurement precision on VEL-9. Sufficient to detect Earth-mass planets in the habitable zone. Limited by stellar granulation noise floor.

Orbital Resonances & Stability

The VEL-9 system exhibits a remarkable chain of near-resonant orbital periods, particularly among the inner four planets. VEL-9 b, c, d, and e participate in a 1:2:4:8 period ratio chain — each planet orbits roughly twice as slowly as its inner neighbor. This architecture suggests a history of convergent migration in the protoplanetary disk, with the planets locking into resonance as they spiraled inward.

The outer planets, g and h, occupy a 2:5 resonance (period ratio 2.5:1), a configuration that is dynamically stable over gigayear timescales. The wide spacing between f and g (period ratio ~4.3) creates a dynamical "gap" — a region where additional planets could theoretically exist but are not observed. This gap may be a fossil signature of a past instability that ejected or collided intermediate bodies.

"The resonance chain isn't perfect. The deviations from exact integer ratios — a few percent here, a fraction of a percent there — are where the history lives. Perfect resonances are theoretical; real systems remember their formation." — Prof. Kaito Mendes, Celestial Mechanics Group, VEL-9 Survey (Cycle 29)

Key Dynamical Features

  • Laplace-like resonance: b-c-d-e chain with three-body arguments librating around 0°
  • Secular resonances: g and h exchange eccentricity on ~12,000-year cycles
  • Kozai-Lidov cycles: Suppressed by coplanarity (mutual inclinations < 0.5°)
  • Tidal damping: Significant only for VEL-9 b (lava world) and c (runaway greenhouse)
04 · Planetary Anatomy

VEL-9 e: The Archetype

Click the hotspots to explore the internal structure and atmospheric processes of the system's most Earth-like world. VEL-9 e is a 1.3 Earth-mass terrestrial planet with a temperate climate, active geology, and a nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere.

Comparative Planetology: VEL-9 e vs. Earth

VEL-9 e is often called "Earth's older cousin" — a label that captures both the similarity and the crucial differences. At 6.2 billion years old, the planet has had 1.7 billion more years of geological evolution than Earth. Its interior has cooled further; its magnetic field is weaker; its tectonics slower. But its atmosphere has stabilized into a composition remarkably close to our own.

Similarities

  • N2-O2 atmosphere (~78/21%)
  • Surface liquid water (oceans cover 63%)
  • Active silicate weathering cycle
  • Dipole magnetic field (0.4× Earth)
  • Obliquity 22.3° (Earth: 23.4°)

Differences

  • Rotation period: 28.4 hours (slower)
  • Surface gravity: 1.12 g
  • Mean surface temp: 18°C (Earth: 15°C)
  • No large moon — axial stability from resonances
  • CO2 at 280 ppm (pre-industrial Earth)

The Biosignature Question

VEL-9 e's atmosphere shows a persistent disequilibrium: O2 and CH4 coexist at levels that, on Earth, require continuous biological production. The Survey detected this signal in Cycle 22 via direct imaging spectroscopy. However, abiotic pathways cannot be fully excluded — particularly photolytic O2 buildup from a steam atmosphere early in the planet's history, followed by hydrogen escape.

The debate remains open. The next observational milestone is the detection (or non-detection) of CH3Cl (methyl chloride) or N2O (nitrous oxide), which would strongly favor a biological source. The Survey's Cycle 50 coronagraph upgrade may reach the required sensitivity.

"We have been here before — Mars in the 1970s, exoplanet atmospheres in the 2020s. The universe is generous with ambiguities. Our job is not to force an answer, but to build instruments that make the question sharper." — Dr. Elara Voss, Cycle 42

Reading the Comparisons

The comparison tool reveals patterns that tables obscure. When you slide between VEL-9 d and e — the "twin" temperate terrestrials — the internal structure view shows nearly identical mantle thicknesses and core mass fractions. But the atmospheric profile tells a different story: d's atmosphere is thinner (0.6 bar vs 1.1 bar), drier, and shows a stronger CO2 signature. The surface view reveals why: d receives 34% more stellar flux, driving a more vigorous hydrological cycle that has, over billions of years, sequestered more carbon into carbonate rocks.

Contrast this with the c vs e greenhouse comparison. VEL-9 c receives 2.1× Earth's insolation — enough to trigger a runaway greenhouse. Its surface temperature exceeds 450°C, its atmosphere is 92 bar of almost pure CO2, and its surface is a global volcanic plain refreshed by catastrophic resurfacing events every few hundred million years. The same planetary mass, the same stellar system, a different orbital distance — and the result is a world that shares almost nothing with its temperate sibling.

The Ocean World Boundary

VEL-9 f sits at the outer edge of the conservative habitable zone. At 1.8 Earth masses, it has held onto a massive water envelope — estimated at 5–10% of planetary mass by weight, compared to Earth's 0.02%. The result is a global ocean hundreds of kilometers deep, with high-pressure ice phases (Ice VI, Ice VII) forming a mantle between the liquid ocean and the silicate mantle below.

This structure has profound implications for habitability. The ice mantle blocks geochemical exchange between the ocean and the rocky interior — no hydrothermal vents, no mineral nutrient cycling. Without a mechanism to replenish electron donors and acceptors, any biosphere would be limited to the thin photic zone at the surface, starved of the chemical energy that powers Earth's deep biosphere.

06 · Glossary & Methods

Terms, Techniques & Conventions

This atlas uses standard exoplanet nomenclature and a few survey-specific conventions. Key terms are marked inline with dotted underlines — hover or focus for definitions.

Nomenclature

VEL-9
The host star. Also designated HD 19876, HIP 14821, Gaia DR4 234567890123456789.
VEL-9 b–h
Planets in order of discovery, not orbital distance. Orbital order: b, c, d, e, f, g, h (coincidentally aligned in this system).
Cycle
One Earth year of survey operations. Cycle 1 began 2024-01-15 (Earth calendar).
PRV
Precision Radial Velocity. The primary mass-determination method for non-transiting planets.
HZ
Habitable Zone. "Conservative HZ" = Kopparapu et al. 2013 boundaries (runaway greenhouse / maximum greenhouse).

Measurement Conventions

  • All masses are true masses (not M sin i), derived from combined PRV + astrometry.
  • Radii from direct imaging (g, h) or mass-radius relations calibrated on transiting systems (b–f). Uncertainties: ±8–15%.
  • Equilibrium temperatures assume Bond albedo 0.3, full heat redistribution. Actual surface temps vary by atmosphere.
  • Stellar flux in Earth-units (S⊕ = 1361 W/m²).
  • Atmospheric compositions from retrieval on direct-imaging spectra (g, h) or transmission/emission spectroscopy (b–f, where geometry permits).

Honest Epistemics

This atlas distinguishes three tiers of knowledge:

Measured

Orbital elements, true masses, stellar parameters, direct-imaging spectra of g/h. Reported with formal uncertainties.

Inferred

Radii of b–f (mass-radius models), surface temperatures (climate models), interior structures (equation-of-state models). Model-dependent.

Speculative

Biosignature assessments, habitability judgments, formation histories. Plausible narratives consistent with data — not conclusions.
