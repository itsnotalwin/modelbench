Fieldwork · Sitting as infrastructure

Who gets
to sit?

On a wet Tuesday in November we counted benches — not to judge taste, but to see where a city makes staying possible, and where it quietly tells some people to keep moving.

The queue at the market was thirty people long. The bench beside it held three shopping bags, a child’s scooter and nobody at all. A city can be full of seats and still give almost nobody a place to sit.

So we walked. For seven wet days in early November we visited six districts, found every bench we could see from a pavement, and scored its frame, slats, fixings, legs and arms on a 0–100 scale. We noted shade at one o’clock, distance to the nearest bus stop, and how many people sat down between eight and nine on a Tuesday.

01 Why we counted

A bench is small, cheap and politically boring. That is why it matters. It decides whether a parent can rest while a toddler eats, whether an older person can wait for a bus without leaning on a railing, whether a night-shift worker can close their eyes for ten minutes without being asked to move on.

Our argument is simple: look at where benches are sound, shaded and close to transit, and you see who a city expects to linger. A broken slat, shipped to a district that already feels hurried, is a signal about whose rest counts.

How to read this feature

Dataset: 36 benches (6 per district) — a constructed, illustrative audit. It is not a citywide survey and does not describe a real city. Units, definitions and limits are listed in the method notes after the article. Every chart can also be read from the table — you do not need colour or hover.

02 How we counted

We defined a bench as any fixed, outdoor, two-person-or-more seat we could reach on foot, excluding café chairs. In each district — North Loop, Central Market, Riverside, Harbor, Hilltop and Industrial Edge — we walked at least three streets and took the first six benches found. That convenience sample is a limitation; we flag it below.

Each bench was scored for Condition Index (0–100; frame, slats, fixings, base, armrests × 20) and Shade % (seat area in shadow at 13:00, in 10% steps). We paced metres to the nearest bus stop and counted sits per hour (people seen seated, 08:00–09:00 Tuesday). Primary issue is the single worst fault, or “None”.

03 The map of repair

The spread is stark. North Loop, refitted two years ago, averages 84 on condition. Industrial Edge, never refitted, averages 38. Same budget line, almost opposite experiences of the same furniture.

Age does not explain everything. Hilltop’s benches are old (mean 19 years) yet score 71. Harbor’s are newer (mean 9 years) yet scatter: two pristine, two already loose. Maintenance is doing real work here.

04 Shade and the midday test

Condition says whether a bench can hold you. At midday, shade says whether you would want to be held. Riverside averaged 62% shade; Industrial Edge 18%. For someone with a heat condition, a baby, or a long wait, that is the difference between a five-minute rest and none at all.

The scatter below plots all 36 benches. The top-right cluster is both sound and shadowed; the bottom-left is neither. North Loop and Riverside sit near trees and arcades. Industrial Edge faces open asphalt.

05 The repair backlog

If condition is the score, the backlog is the to-do list. Broken slats dominate in Industrial Edge and Hilltop; loose fixings — the bench that rocks when you weight it — dominate in Harbor. “None” appears only in North Loop and Central Market, meaning no structural fault, not visual perfection.

The stacked bars add to six per district. Use compare to place any two districts side by side; the delta is what a budget meeting would actually need.

06 What this cannot prove

We cannot prove that prestige drives spending, that shade causes use, or that any figure describes a real place. The audit is constructed and composite; the convenience sample may favour main streets over estates and parks. Shade was estimated coarsely and “sits per hour” is one Tuesday snapshot. Treat this as a rehearsal for a real audit, not a substitute.

07 A fairer bench standard

Suppose a city published a plain test: within 100 m of a bus stop, seats three people, scores 75+ on condition, offers 40% midday shade. Only six of our 36 benches would pass — five of them in just two districts.

That is the point of a standard: boring furniture becomes an auditable promise. The next audit should be larger, stratified, repeated across seasons, and done with people who wait. Until then, tell us which bench made a day better, and why.

Method, units and limits (concise editorial notes)

  • Sample: 36 benches, 6 per district, convenience sample (first benches found on foot). Not random; not representative of any city.
  • Condition Index: 0–100 composite (frame, slats, fixings, base, armrests × 20). Two raters; disagreements averaged. Units: points.
  • Shade %: Estimated share of seat in shadow at 13:00 solar time in 10% steps on a dry day. Units: percent.
  • Transit distance: Paced metres along pavement to nearest scheduled bus stop. Units: metres.
  • Sits/hr: Count of unique people observed seated 08:00–09:00 on a Tuesday. Units: people per hour (snapshot).
  • Primary issue: Single worst fault (broken slats, loose fixings, missing bolts, cosmetic, base damage, none).
  • Status: Constructed/illustrative dataset for this feature. No external claims are cited because none are made about real places.
  • Storage: Your district selection is remembered locally (localStorage) so the page restores on reload. Nothing leaves the browser.
