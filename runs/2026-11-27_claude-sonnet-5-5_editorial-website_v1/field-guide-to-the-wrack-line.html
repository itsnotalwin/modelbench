A Field Guide to the Wrack Line

Every high tide writes a sentence in kelp, cork and bone along the sand. Five things worth stooping for, and what each one is trying to tell you.

By Tomas Ibarra · 19 November 2026 · 4 min read

The wrack line is the long, ragged seam where the sea stops. It is also the shore’s slowest-moving archive: a strip of whatever the last high tide could not carry any further, sorted by weight and buoyancy into something close to a sentence. Walk it with your head down and a pocket for your finds. Here are five that repay the stoop.

No. 1The mermaid’s purse

A black, leathery rectangle with a curled horn at each corner, about the size of a matchbox. It is the empty egg case of a skate or ray. The horns once anchored it in weed; the slit at one end is where a hatchling left. Held to the light, the old ones turn translucent amber.

No. 2Cuttlebone

The pale, chalky oval is the internal shell of a cuttlefish, a buoyancy tank of fine layers that once held gas. Cuttlefish die after spawning, so a drift of bones in season suggests a spawning ground not far offshore.

No. 3The kelp holdfast

The gnarled root-ball of a kelp plant does not feed it; it only grips. Torn loose by a storm, it arrives with a small zoo of worms, tiny crabs and brittle stars still tucked inside. Shake it gently over a tray of seawater and watch them emerge, then put it back.

Humans are the other tide.

No. 4The lost glove

Count the human things: gloves, bottle caps, the heel of a flip-flop. Plastic travels as well as weed does, and the sea sorts it with the same indifference. A strand that is mostly plastic tells you a harbour or river mouth lies somewhere upwind, and a date moulded into a cap tells you the earliest it could have set out.

No. 5The line itself

The last find is not an object. Note where today’s wrack sits against the dark stain of yesterday’s and the bleached, dry line of last month’s storm. Three lines, three tides, three kinds of weather. Photograph the same stretch from the same spot every month and you have a record no gauge will give you. Leave the living things where they are, and take only what is clearly empty.
