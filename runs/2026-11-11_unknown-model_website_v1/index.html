Tone, told in dots.

Every grey you have ever seen in print or on a screen is a bargain: a short list of ink marks, arranged so the eye agrees to see something softer. Ordered is a field guide to that bargain, with every method running live in your browser.

02 — The problem

A printing press can only do one thing: put ink on paper, or leave it off. It has no grey at all. Every tone you have ever seen in print is a trick of spacing: dots small enough and close enough that the eye averages them into something soft. The craft was always in deciding where the dots go.

Threshold

Compare every pixel with one fixed value. It is fast and brutal: anything near the midpoint is lost to a single decision, which is why most of the grey disappears.

Ordered 8×8

A fixed 8 × 8 matrix of thresholds, the Bayer matrix from Bryce Bayer's 1973 paper. Each pixel is tested against its own position, which gives a regular, engraved texture.

Floyd–Steinberg

Each pixel's rounding error is pushed onto its unvisited neighbours, so the average tone survives and no fixed pattern shows. Robert Floyd and Louis Steinberg, 1976.

Atkinson

Bill Atkinson's variant spreads only three quarters of each error. Highlights stay clean and shadows stay open, a look closely associated with the original Macintosh.

04 — Step wedge

Eleven steps from paper to ink, and the press gets the middle wrong.

Printers calibrate with a wedge of flat tones. Ink spreads as it lands, so every dot prints a little larger than it was drawn. That is dot gain, and it is worst in the midtones. Turn it up to see what a press does to an intention.

05 — History

A short history of the dot.

Each date below is a moment when someone decided that a grey could be built out of something else.

  1. 1839

    The daguerreotype is announced

    Daguerre's process is made public in Paris. Photographs arrive as continuous tone, and the presses of the day cannot print that, so the problem of grey begins.

  2. 1852

    Talbot patents photo-engraving through a screen

    William Henry Fox Talbot describes photographing through fine screens or veils, breaking an image into a pattern that a press can ink. This is the ancestor of the halftone.

  3. 1879

    Ben-Day tints

    Benjamin Henry Day's patent for mechanical tints lets illustrators shade drawings with printed dot patterns, rather than hand-stippling every tone.

  4. 1880

    The first halftone in a newspaper

    On 4 March, the New York Daily Graphic prints Stephen H. Horgan's photograph “A Scene in Shantytown”, made through a fine screen so that the picture is built from dots.

  5. 1973

    Bayer's ordered matrix

    Bryce Bayer's paper on the two-level rendition of continuous-tone pictures sets out an ordered arrangement of thresholds that spreads dots evenly. Ordered dither starts here.

  6. 1976

    Error diffusion

    Robert Floyd and Louis Steinberg show that pushing each pixel's rounding error onto its unvisited neighbours produces convincing tones with no obvious pattern.

  7. 1984

    Dither arrives on the desktop

    Bill Atkinson's variant of error diffusion lets one-bit screens show photographs, a look closely associated with the original Macintosh.

  8. 1987

    Blue noise

    Robert Ulichney's Digital Halftoning popularises blue noise: random-looking dots kept evenly apart, so the pattern is hard for the eye to find.
