01 — The sky

We don’t sell “stargazing”. We sell darkness.

Anyone can put a telescope in a garden. Darkness is harder. It took us four years of refusing floodlights, negotiating with the farm next door, and learning to live by red torchlight — but the reward is a sky you can navigate by. This page checks the sky for the night you’ll actually be here, because a good host tells you the truth before you drive up the hill.

Everything below is calculated live in your browser from today’s date — the moon tonight, the meteor showers this season, and the difference between our sky and the one you left in town. If the moon is full, we’ll say so. If a meteor peak falls on your Friday, you’ll know before you book, not after you arrive.

    03 — How a night works

    Dusk to dawn, at walking pace.

    Nobody needs a packed itinerary under a dark sky. The night runs itself once your eyes adjust — about twenty minutes — and we just keep the kettle full and the telescopes warm. Times shift with the season (a summer night is a short one, and in June we start later and drink more tea), but the shape of the evening stays the same.

    16:00

    Arrive

    Park at the barn, not in the field. Tea, a route to your room, and a warning about the step onto the deck.

    18:00

    Supper

    Friday only. One long table, whatever June cooked, phones in the basket by the door — optional, but almost everyone does it.

    19:30

    Sky walk

    90 minutes on the ridge with Mara. Constellations, the Milky Way’s dark lanes, and how to read a star chart in red light.

    21:00

    Observing

    The dome opens. The Dobsonian finds Saturn, the Moon’s craters, the Andromeda Galaxy — in that order, usually, because no one can resist Saturn.

    23:30

    Hot chocolate

    Back inside for cocoa and a debrief in the kitchen. The sauna slots run until midnight on Fridays.

    07:30

    Breakfast

    Porridge, eggs, sourdough. On Saturday, telescopes are still up for the Moon if she’s late-rising.

    10:30

    Leave

    Slowly. Your atlas is printed and posted within the week. The ridge will still be here; the sky, annoyingly, will have moved on.

    In winter the whole timetable moves forward by two hours and the sky gets dramatically better. In June, sunset is after 21:00 and we observe until the sky just greys. Either way, you’ll see more in one night than most people see in a year of streetlights.

    05 — Who’s behind it

    Three people, one stubborn ridge.

    Duskfield isn’t a brand that bought a farmhouse. It’s three households’ worth of decisions made slowly, in rain, by people who live here. We answer every enquiry ourselves — usually Mara, usually with a cup of tea in hand — and if we think your dates would be a bad idea (full moon, cloud forecast, room too small for the dog), we’ll tell you and suggest a better Friday.

    M

    Mara Ellison

    Host · Astronomer

    Twelve years presenting planetarium shows in York and Newcastle before she noticed she spent every clear night driving out to find dark skies. She built the dome’s star charts, runs the sky walk, and can find the Andromeda Galaxy with her eyes closed. She will not admit to the eyes-closed part.

    “My job is mostly to get out of the way once your eyes adjust. The sky does the rest.”
    T

    Tom Ellison

    Joiner · Builder

    Mara’s brother. Built the dome, the sauna, the deck, and — twice — the telescope mount, after the first one met a gust of moor wind in 2021. Everything at Duskfield is over-engineered by about 30%, which on this ridge is exactly right. He also fixes the roof, the stove, and the gate.

    “If it can’t survive a February gale and a curious guest, it doesn’t go up.”
    J

    June Park

    Cook · Gardener

    June cooks Friday supper, both breakfasts, and the rhubarb crumble that has its own fan mail. She runs the kitchen garden, keeps the bees (two hives, one grumpy), and has strong, stated opinions about the order of telescope targets. She is usually right about the food.

    “Nobody sees stars well on an empty stomach. That’s a scientific fact now.”
    No floodlights, ever

    Every exterior light on the ridge is amber, hooded, or red after 21:00. House rules are in the welcome pack, and guests actually love them by night two.

    Red light at night

    Torches, door lights and even the kettle’s little LED are red or covered. It sounds extreme. Twenty minutes later, you’ll never go back.

    Small on purpose

    Four rooms, eight guests, one table. We turn people away every month rather than add a fifth room and lose the dark. That’s the whole business model.
