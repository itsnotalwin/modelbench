NOCTURNE · Vol. II · Night Sky

The sky after the supermarket

Brightness is the cheapest form of signage and the most expensive form of night. On three invented parks, darkness was kept as a civic asset — and the birds noticed before the council did.

The car park is the darkest part of the night here, which is the point. At 22:41 in the fictional Vale Park — a 38-hectare invented reserve ringing an invented village of 9,000 — the supermarket behind the village has already dimmed to its midnight setting. The sky above the car park is not black. It is the colour of a television left on in another room: blue-grey, patient, full of stars that are not the brightest ones. The wardens call this “shelf light”, and they budget for it the way others budget for water. In this account every brightness figure is invented, but the unit is real: the Bortle class, a 1-to-9 scale invented by a real astronomer, which the article uses as a ruler for fictional skies.

The argument is that darkness is infrastructure. We treat light as a service and darkness as an absence, then we wonder why we cannot govern the bill. The supermarket’s light is not a luxury; it is rented visibility, and the rent is paid in migrants that miss their season, in moths that find the wrong lamp, in children who grow up believing that the Milky Way is a photograph. The parks in this story are laboratories for a different accounting: a place where the lumen is counted like the euro, a place where dimming is signed for, not merely suggested.

“A dark park is not a park without light. It is a park that has decided what light is for.”
Margin — Bortle classes 1–3 are genuinely dark skies; 4–5 are suburban; 6+ are bright. The classes here are invented for three parks; the scale itself is a real reference.
Chapter One

What the night does to a body that flies

Biology first, because biology is the creditor. The invented study that the article follows — 14 April to 9 October, 32 moth traps, 88 nights — recorded 1,940 individuals in Vale Park. Of those, 41% were captured within 10 metres of the white car-park floodlight. The floodlight was not the brightest thing in the park; the village streetlights were brighter. The floodlight was simply the one that could be turned off. After dimming to 30% at midnight, the catch within 10 metres fell to 22% and the total night count rose by 18%. The numbers are invented. The mechanism is not: a moth that navigates by moonlight has no way to know that a 4,000-kelvin floodlight is not the moon. It assumes, and assumption is what light pollution exploits.

An ecological trap is a place that looks like habitat and behaves like a threat. In this article, the bright edge of a dark park is the example.

The night birds tell a slower version of the same story. The invented warden log for Vale Park records the first nightjar of the season on 19 May, 11 days earlier than the pre-dimming average. One date does not prove a trend, and the article does not claim one. It claims something smaller and therefore stronger: dimming is one of the few conservation acts that can be done with a timer. You do not need a grant to change a setting. You need the setting.

Chapter Two

The economics of a lumen

Economics second, because economics is the excuse. The supermarket that lights the Vale car park pays for the light twice: once in electricity, once in the brand. The invented bill is 19,400 lumen-hours per night for the floodlights, of which 61% is spill light that lands on the park rather than the cars. The manager’s maths is exact: dimming to 30% saves 11,600 lumen-hours and costs, in the story, zero complaints and one improved star field. The council’s maths is the interesting one. A dark-sky certification — invented here as “Good Night” — brings 4,200 visitors a year who stay for dinner and leave for the stars. They spend €31 on average in the village. The park gains the dark; the village gains the dinner. Both gain, which is why the story needs three parks, not one: to show that the gain is not automatic.

The table is the comparison that makes the essay. Quarry Park is the counterexample: same region, brighter sky, fewer visitors, later birds. It is not a tragedy; it is a price. The dark park is not cheaper to run — dimming gear, certification, wardens — but it is cheaper to defend, because darkness has a constituency that floodlights do not. People will travel to see stars. Nobody travels to see a car park.

Chapter Three

The park that kept its night

What does keeping look like? At Vale it looks like a timer. At Mire it looks like a shield — a baffle added to each lamp so that light goes down and not out. At Quarry it looks like a decision not taken: the housing that was promised a bright edge was offered a dim one, and the estate agent, in the fiction, learned to sell the dark as the view. None of these is a technology breakthrough. All of them are administrative ones. The article’s thesis is that darkness is not lost the way wilderness is lost, by machines; it is lost the way budgets are lost, by settings. A setting can be changed back.

“Darkness is the only part of the park the supermarket cannot buy. That is why it is worth keeping.”
Chapter Four

Coda: the sky you are standing under

At midnight the car park steps down to 30%. The wardens do not make an announcement. The moths make one, in the only language they have: the edge of the trap thins, the fields fill, the nightjar comes home earlier. The village, without knowing it, has become a lighthouse that points down. This feature invents its parks and its prices, but it does not invent the choice. Every town with a supermarket has the choice to dim. That the choice is made in a settings menu rather than a consultation is, if anything, the good news: the night can be saved by a timer, and the timer can be signed for by anyone who has read this far. Set it for midnight. Look up.

Method

All park names, areas, budgets, catches and visitor figures are invented for narrative consistency. The Bortle scale, the moth-navigation mechanism and the dimming arithmetic are real concepts, represented here with invented numbers. Nothing here is a field report. No real park is described as Vale, Mire or Quarry.

1. Bortle classes are real; Vale (3.4) and Mire (3.8) are invented representative values. Village centre 7.0 is invented but consistent with the story’s glow.
2. Moth navigation by moonlight is a real hypothesis; the 41% → 22% diagnostic is invented for this article.
3. Lumen-hour budgets are invented. Spill-share figures are invented and sum consistently with the charts.
