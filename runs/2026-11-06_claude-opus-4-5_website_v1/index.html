  1. Umbra · a field manual for the shadow of the Moon

    On 2 August 2027 the Sun goes out for six minutes and twenty-three seconds.

    The longest total eclipse over land until 2114. Scroll to run it from first contact to last, and watch what it does to this page.

  2. C1 · First contact

    A bite out of the Sun.

    The Moon touches the western limb. We have dotted it in for you; in the real sky it is invisible, lit from behind. For the next hour almost nothing seems to happen.

  3. C1 → C2 · Deep partial

    Three-quarters gone, and barely dimmer.

    Your pupils widen and quietly cancel the loss. The light turns thin and silvery, shadows sharpen at their edges. It feels like a cloud that isn’t there.

  4. The last percent

    Still daytime.

    A hair of photosphere is thousands of times brighter than the full Moon: bright enough to blind, far too bright for the corona. This is all Aswan, 200 km south of Luxor, will get.

  5. C2 · Second contact

    Beads, then a diamond.

    The final light breaks into beads through valleys on the Moon’s edge, then a single blazing point. When it’s gone, and only then, glasses off.

  6. Totality

    The sky has an off switch.

    The corona appears: the Sun’s outer atmosphere, over a million degrees and about as bright as the full Moon. Stars come out. Every horizon glows like sunset. At Luxor this lasts 6 minutes 22 seconds.

  7. C3 · Third contact

    Glasses on. Now.

    A bead flares on the opposite edge, then the diamond ring again. The Sun is dangerous to look at the instant it returns.

  8. C3 → C4 · Waning partial

    The long way back.

    The Moon takes another hour to slide away. Most people stop watching at third contact. They are already talking about the next one.

  9. C4 · Fourth contact

    Where will you stand?

    The shadow is up to 258 km wide. Outside it there is no totality at all, only a partial eclipse, however deep.

02 — Light

Ninety-nine percent is still daytime.

Your eyes work on a logarithmic scale. They hide most of a partial eclipse from you, and the last sliver of Sun is still bright enough to blind you. In 2027 Aswan, 200 km south of Luxor, gets 99.8% of the Sun covered. It will not get dark there. Drag the slider and watch.

      06 — Field notes

      Eight things to know before the light goes.

      01

      Partial is not total.

      Anywhere outside the path, however close, you get a partial eclipse: interesting through a filter, unremarkable without one. The gap between 99.8% and 100% is the gap between a dim afternoon and the strangest thing you will ever see. Get inside the line.

      02

      Filters for every partial second.

      Look at the partial phases only through solar viewers made to ISO 12312-2. Sunglasses, any number of pairs, are not filters. Nor are smoked glass, exposed film or a phone screen. Cameras, binoculars and telescopes need proper solar filters on the front.

      03

      Naked eyes for totality only.

      When the last bead of sunlight has gone, take the glasses off: the corona is safe to look at, and invisible through a filter. The moment the first flash returns at third contact, they go straight back on.

      04

      Look down.

      Gaps between leaves work as pinhole cameras, and the ground under a tree fills with small crescent Suns. A colander or a straw hat does the same.

      05

      Watch for shadow bands.

      In the minute before and after totality, faint ripples can race across pale ground. That is the last sliver of sunlight scintillating through the atmosphere. Lay out a white sheet and keep half an eye on it.

      06

      Leave the camera.

      If this is your first totality, don’t spend it behind a screen. A phone can’t see what your eyes will. If you must, set it up beforehand on a tripod, start it running, and look up.

      07

      Choose sky over seconds.

      Weather beats duration. The Nile valley in August is about as reliable as skies get; the Strait is good odds, with sea mist as the usual spoiler. Have a plan B you can drive to the night before.

      08

      Feel the edges.

      The temperature falls by several degrees. Birds go quiet. The horizon glows in every direction at once, because you are standing in a shadow about 250 km across, and beyond its rim it is still afternoon.
