The Undertext: Reading What Was Scraped Off

A palimpsest is not a scribal accident. It is a page with two lives—and sometimes a third. How parchment was recycled, how chemistry found the first text, and why multispectral imaging rewrote what we can read in history’s scraped sheets.

There is a particular pleasure in finding a sentence its first readers tried to destroy. On a shelf in a monastery, inside a prayer book, under a century of prayers laid over it, a phrase from a dead tongue holds its breath. We call the object a palimpsest—from Greek palímpsēstos, “scraped again”—and the hidden first text its undertext. But the name describes a technique, not a rarity. Every document is a palimpsest of some kind: written on something that held something before, and destined, usually, to hold something after.

Scraped again

Medieval scribes worked with an expensive material. Parchment, prepared animal skin, cost far more than ink, and manuscripts were capital. When a codex was no longer needed—a superseded liturgy, a finished ledger, a student’s exercise—the sensible thing was not to throw it away but to scrape it. A curved blade and an abrasive, traditionally pumice, removed the ink; the surface was smoothed, sometimes re-stretched; a fresh text was copied on top. The first writing was seldom obliterated perfectly. Ink soaks into skin, and scraping removes only what sits on the surface.1

Medieval copyists knew what they were undoing. Erasures inside a single manuscript—a word scraped out and rewritten in place—are a different craft, a rasura, and scholars read them as carefully as the final text. But a whole-page palimpsest is something stranger: a document deliberately forgetting itself so it can remember something else. The economics are the point. Paper displaced parchment and palimpsests became rare, but the category never disappears; we will argue that it cannot.

Gall-nut ink, iron-gall ink, and carbon ink respond differently to light and reagent. Most surviving palimpsests are fights between two inks on one skin.

Two lives of a page

The Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus is the cleanest illustration. A fourth-century Greek manuscript of Scripture—one of the great uncials—was scraped in the sixth century and reused for sermons by Ephrem the Syrian. The Bible survives underneath, letter by letter, unevenly erased; the sermons sit on top. Neither text was meant to accompany the other. They share a skin the way two tenants share a wall.2 A reader of the sixth century would have seen only sermons. A reader of the twentieth century, armed with chemistry, can read both—and must then decide what it means that one was hidden.

Often the undertext is not another literary work at all. It is a ledger, a tax roll, a land register, a student’s grammar exercise, or a half-finished draft scraped for reuse. The second life of a page says nothing reliable about the worth of the first. What survives scraping is an accident of ink chemistry and scribal thoroughness. Scholars call the recovered layer the undertext precisely because we cannot always tell, from the visible page, where it begins.

The word undertext is older than the instruments that find it. Critics used it for the text beneath a translation long before anyone could photograph a scraped page.

The Archimedes case

No palimpsest has done more for history than the Archimedes Palimpsest. A tenth-century Byzantine scribe scraped a collection of Archimedes’ works and reused the parchment for a prayer book; the prayers are written at right angles to the Greek, which is why they are called palimpsest in every sense at once. In 1906 the undertext was identified by the Danish scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg, who worked from photographs in candlelight. He recovered texts then known only in later Arabic translations—and one work, the Method of Mechanical Theorems, previously unknown at all.3

The manuscript vanished from scholarly sight, resurfaced on the art market in 1998, and was bought by an anonymous collector who loaned it for study. Conservators at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore stabilized it—some pages had been pulled apart, others re-glued with wax—and a team of imaging scientists, librarians, and classicists went back over what Heiberg could only see.4 The palimpsest turned out to contain, besides the Method:

  • On the Equilibrium of Planes
  • On Spirals
  • Measurement of a Circle
  • On the Sphere and Cylinder
  • On Floating Bodies
  • The Stomachion (a combinatorial fragment)

What Heiberg read at a desk in Constantinople, a century later a synchrotron could read in the margins he missed. The Method is the philosophical heart of the find: Archimedes showing how he discovered results, weighing infinitely thin slices on an imaginary balance, before proving them rigorously. A scraped prayer book preserved the workshop of mathematics.

The undertext is not a ghost. It is evidence—ink on skin, waiting for light of the right wavelength.

Palimpsest, editorial note

Seeing through ink

For two hundred years after Heiberg, reading an undertext was slow chemistry. Reagents applied to a page could darken faded ink; ultraviolet light revealed differences in fluorescence between inks and parchment; scholars traced what they saw onto acetate. Every technique risked damage, and nothing could recover strokes whose ink had been scraped clean through.

The turn of this century changed the instruments, not the principle. Multispectral imaging photographs a page repeatedly, under controlled lighting, across the spectrum—ultraviolet, visible bands, infrared—and combines the exposures computationally. The undertext’s ink and the second text’s ink absorb and reflect differently at different wavelengths; software separates what the eye flattens. X-ray fluorescence can map the elements in an ink—iron in one layer, lead in another—without touching the page at all.5 Nothing is restored; nothing is added to the manuscript. The reading is new; the object is untouched.

A reconstruction is not a claim about what a scribe meant. It is a claim about what ink remains. Palimpsest reports the difference.

A layered reading

The history of the palimpsest keeps turning one fact over. Pages are reused because materials are expensive; scraping is imperfect because ink is stubborn; hidden text is recovered because measurement improves. Nothing here is a metaphor imported from elsewhere. The word names a physical process, and the physical process keeps producing history.

Our editorial stance follows. Read every document as a layered object. Ask what it was written on and what was there before; what was scraped away and by whom; what has been added at the edges. Do not mistake the visible surface for the whole. And when a reconstruction is offered—by a nineteenth-century scholar with reagents or a twenty-first-century team with a synchrotron—ask what was measured, under what conditions, and what remains honestly unrecovered. The undertext is not a secret waiting for the right key. It is evidence waiting for the right care.
