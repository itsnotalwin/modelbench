Marginalia: The Reader’s Second Text

The blank margin is an invitation. From Pliny’s scribes to digital highlights: what readers have always written, and what they lose when the page disappears.

A book arrives with margins already cut, blank, waiting. Nothing in the object says leave this space empty, and yet the emptiness is the point: the margin is where the second text gets written. Marginalia—notes, corrections, translations, objections, jokes, citations, and small drawings in the space around the main text—are not damage to a book. They are the visible record that someone read it. On inspected library copies, they are often the most human thing on the page.

Written at the edge

Annotation is as old as writing on reusable surfaces. Medieval scriptoria filled glossae—brief explanations, cross-references, and corrections—into the margins of biblical and patristic manuscripts, sometimes so densely that later readers added still another layer between the lines. Interlinear glosses on the Glossa Ordinaria, marginal commentary in scholastic disputations, corrections slipped in by scribes who caught their own errors mid-copy: the margin accretes. The text was never only what the author wrote.

The Romans complained about exactly this. The natural historian Pliny, no friend of careless copyists, groused that hired scribes polluted books with their own additions and interpolations; elsewhere he urged readers to mark passages worth remembering. Both observations are the same fact seen from two sides: a page is shared property once it is read. The reader’s mark is an editorial act, however humble—this matters, this is wrong, this I must answer.1

A note in this margin is itself an example: this essay reads the history of margins from inside one.

The blank margin as invitation

Printing did not end annotation; it scaled it. When Aldus Manutius issued his small octavo editions in Venice in the 1490s, he argued—in the preface to his Pietro Bembo editions and in his famous instructions to readers—for compact books and wide margins, so a reader could mark a passage without crowding the text. Pocket format and blank space went together: a book small enough to carry, opened enough to write in.2 The scholarly apparatus of footnotes and critical editions later formalized what readers had been doing informally: gathering variants, sources, and objections at the edge.

The commonplace book extended the margin across pages. From the Renaissance through the nineteenth century, readers copied out sentences they admired, filed them by topic, and added their own reflections; Samuel Johnson, Francis Bacon, and Hannah More kept them, and John Locke wrote a treatise on the practice. The margin’s private note and the commonplace’s public excerpt are two tempos of the same habit: reading produces writing.

Reading as writing

The claim that reading is a kind of writing is old, and the margins are its proof. Walter Benjamin argued that the reader’s task in reading The Arabian Nights was not to absorb a finished work but to continue it—that reception itself is a creative act—and he collected the critical notes of earlier readers as evidence of a life lived alongside books. Libraries hold whole copies whose value is the annotation: Walt Whitman’s reading, marginalia in a copy of the Oxford English Dictionary, a reader’s careful scholarly apparatus in pencil. Inspected copies are not damaged goods; they are collaborations frozen in place.3

There is a palimpsest structure here, small and repeated. The author writes; the reader answers in the margin; the next reader reads both. Nothing is scraped away—annotation adds—but the layers are the same idea. Our sister essay on the undertext concerns text recovered by removing what covered it; marginalia is text added at the edge. Both say the visible surface is not the whole document.

A margin is a promise: whatever is printed, there is room for you to answer.

The digital margin

The screen rearranges the economics of margin space. A phone shows no blank margin; annotation moves into a side panel, a popover, or a separate layer that can be toggled. E-readers made highlighting frictionless—a tap marks a phrase—and made the results separable from the page: a highlight can be exported, shared, and sold back to you as “your notes.” Frictionless is not the same as free. A swipe-erasable highlight leaves no physical trace on anything you own; whether that loses something depends on whether the layer is kept, backed up, and yours.4

And a highlight is not a note. Tapping to mark a phrase records that you were struck; writing a sentence in the margin records why. The cheapness of digital marking can flood a text with marks that mean nothing to anyone, including their author, a month later. The printed margin imposes a cost—ink, space, the effort of writing—that quietly filters. The digital margin removes the cost; removing the cost does not remove the value of pausing.

Every remark in this issue’s margins—printed or on screen—is a second text. Read them.

What the margin keeps

What the margin keeps, across fifteen centuries and every change of medium, is the reader. Not the reader as passive receiver but as participant: correcting, objecting, connecting, remembering. A book with no marks has either not been read or been read without leaving a trace, and those are hard to tell apart. The blank margin is the space where reading becomes writing; the filled margin is the evidence that it happened.

So leave room. Whether on paper or glass, whether your note is a sentence or a dot, the habit is the same one the scribes had: say something back. The text was never only what the author wrote.
