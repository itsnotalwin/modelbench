Whole Sun

The Moon’s shadow is already racing across the planet. From here, the Sun is still whole.

T−01:15:070% of the Sun covered

For a few minutes the day forgets itself. Birds go quiet, the horizon turns to dusk in every direction, and the one thing you were taught never to look at becomes the only thing worth looking at.

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so from Earth they look nearly the same size. When the Moon looks a little larger, you get totality. When it looks a little smaller, you get a bright ring instead.

In this model the Moon looks 3 percent wider than the Sun. Scroll distance is stretched around the middle, so totality, which lasts 2 minutes 13 seconds here, takes real effort to pass through.

How to look

Until the last sliver is gone

Use eclipse glasses or a handheld viewer marked ISO 12312-2. Sunglasses, smoked glass and stacked film do not count. Outside the path of totality, the Sun is never safe to watch without a filter.

During totality

Once no bright part of the Sun remains, bare eyes are safe. The corona is faint, wide and shaped by the Sun’s magnetic field. Look for as long as totality lasts, and not a second longer.

When the bead returns

The first spark of light on the far edge is third contact. Put the glasses back on before you look again. Cameras need a solar filter on the lens before and after totality.
