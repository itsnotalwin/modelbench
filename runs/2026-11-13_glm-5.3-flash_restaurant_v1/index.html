01 · The hearth

We built the fire first.
The room grew around it.

In the summer of 2020 we bought nine crates of brick from the old Waddell cannery demolition on McLoughlin — a weekend project that got out of hand. Naiara’s grandmother cooked over oak in a caserío outside Bilbao, where a low fire isn’t a technique, it’s the day’s clock. We stood a nine-foot hearth in a warehouse on Ankeny Street and started cooking for twelve friends a night.

Five years on, the room seats thirty-eight. The fire is still the only thing we trust with the centre of the plate: lamb hung over vine cuttings, leeks buried in ash until they collapse, bread baked against the ovens that warm the back wall. When the woodpile changes, the menu changes — oak in winter, valley apple and cherry all summer.

We shake our own vermouth? No — we steep it, in charred American oak, and pour it from the tap by the door. We pour sidra from a height and catch two fingers. We burn the cheesecake on purpose. Everything else, we leave alone and let the coals decide.

  • The bar is walk-in only — six stools, first come, best seat in the house.
  • Tables of five or more share the long oak and pass everything family-style.
  • Cash, card, and honest appetites. Tipping is shared with the whole room.
  • Tell us what you can’t eat. The fire adapts; it always has.

If the fire looks angry, we wait. It’s almost never angry — it’s hungry.
