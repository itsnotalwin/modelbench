Whose noon is it?

How towns, railways and governments argued their way to one shared clock, one meridian at a time.

Every town had its own noon

For most of history, time was local. A town set its clocks by the sun, and when the sun stood at its highest, it was noon. The sun crosses the sky from east to west, so a town a few hundred kilometres further west, on a different meridian, reached solar noon a little later. Nobody needed a common clock to open a market or plough a field.

The arithmetic is simple and surprising. The Earth turns through 360 degrees in about 24 hours, so each degree of longitude is roughly four minutes of solar time. A journey of a few hundred kilometres east or west can move noon by many minutes. Towns therefore kept local mean time, an average solar day that smoothed out the sun's uneven pace, and it still differed from one town to the next.

Ten minutes between two towns hardly mattered on foot. It mattered a great deal once a train ran between them.

The railway needed one clock

Railways changed the arithmetic. A timetable printed in one town had to make sense in the next, and a station clock had to agree with the train it served. Lines that kept their own local times produced a tangle of conflicting schedules. In 1840 the Great Western Railway began running its timetables on London time, and other companies gradually followed. At first a railway clock was a private agreement, but it proved useful enough that others copied it.

By the late 1840s most British railway companies had settled on Greenwich time, while many towns kept their own time for years longer. The gap between a station clock and a church clock could be the gap between catching a train and missing it.

Two noons in one day

In the United States the problem was continental. Cities and railroads ran on hundreds of local and company times, and a traveller could cross several of them in a single afternoon. On 18 November 1883 the major railroads switched to five standard zones, and many cities reset their clocks to match. In some towns clocks were set back, so noon came round twice, which is why the day is remembered as the day of two noons.

The zones were a railway arrangement before they were law. Congress did not make standard time official until 1918, long after the railroads had made the change.

A meridian for the world

The world needed a single reference line. In October 1884 an International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C., recommended the meridian through Greenwich as the prime meridian, the zero line of longitude, and recommended a day that begins at midnight. Its decisions were recommendations rather than binding treaties, so countries adopted them at their own pace.

Radio made the shared clock audible. From 1924 the BBC broadcast the Greenwich time signal, the six pips, and listeners set their watches by it. In 1972 Coordinated Universal Time began, a scale based on atomic clocks and adjusted with occasional leap seconds so that it stays close to the Earth's rotation.

Zones, offsets and half-hours

The map of time zones is still an argument that never quite ended. Zones follow borders and economies as much as meridians, so a country can sit far from its own solar noon. China keeps a single time across roughly sixty degrees of longitude. Nepal's clocks sit at UTC+5:45 and India's at UTC+5:30, so neither follows a whole-hour line, and both choices show that the map is political as well as astronomical.

Some places move their clocks each spring and autumn, and others never do. Each change is a decision somebody had to make, and that somebody is usually a government rather than the sun.

Glossary

Solar noon
The moment the sun crosses a place's meridian and stands at its highest point in the sky.
Meridian
An imaginary line of constant longitude, running from pole to pole.
Local mean time
Clock time based on the average solar day at a given longitude, which evens out the sun's uneven pace through the year.
Prime meridian
The meridian chosen as zero longitude. The 1884 conference recommended the one through Greenwich.
Time zone
A region that keeps one clock time, usually an offset from a reference time and set by law as often as by longitude.
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time, the reference scale behind most clocks today. It rests on atomic standards and uses occasional leap seconds.
