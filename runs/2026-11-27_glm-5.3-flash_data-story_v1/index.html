Duck Season.

For a century, this city’s electricity demand rose with the kettle and peaked at dusk. Then five gigawatts of solar arrived overhead — and quietly moved the grid’s hardest hour to just after sunset.

01

The rhythm that never moved

Every electricity grid keeps the same diary. Demand climbs as the city wakes, sags through the afternoon, and peaks when people come home and the lights come on. The quietest moment of the day used to be around 4 a.m. — and grids were engineered around exactly that rhythm.

Mean hourly demand, all 366 days of 2010 · shaded band = middle 80% of days · steel = demand measured at the meter

This is the shape the whole system was built for: power stations sized to reach the evening head, plants idling through the tail. Nothing about a city’s day changes quickly. Nothing changed for a hundred years. Then, on the same meter that never moved, something started to.

02

Then the middle fell out

Between 2010 and 2024, Meridian installed roughly five gigawatts of photovoltaics — a million rooftops, carports, and two utility fields. Every panel floods the grid at the same moment. Keep scrolling, or drag the scrubber yourself.

2010 · the baseline

A century-old shape

The amber sliver is solar — barely a rounding error. What the grid buys is what the city uses: a morning shoulder, an afternoon plateau, and an evening peak near 19:00. The night-time tail is the quietest part of the day.

2014 · the leak begins

Roofs start to leak sunshine

Panels don’t ask permission: every clear noon, they simply subtract themselves from demand. Measured at the substation, the middle of the day begins to sag. The morning and evening shoulders barely move — the change is happening only where the sun is.

2019 → 2024 · the belly

Noon becomes the quietest hour

By —, something without precedent happened: the lowest point of the day was no longer the small hours of the night — it was noon, with the sun up. The curve had grown a belly. Grid operators gave the shape a name: the duck.

The belly is seasonal and weekly. It deepens in March–May — strong sun, mild air, and on weekends, offices gone quiet — and vanishes entirely in winter, when the sun is low and the panels are lucky to cover a fifth of the noon load. On — days of 2024, net demand fell below 150 MW for at least an hour: — in total, — of them on weekends. On the deepest ones the city’s meters went negative — more power made than consumed, with nowhere to send it.

05

Weather is the other author

Solar decides when the duck breathes; weather decides how hard. Each point below is one day of 2024 — its hottest hour against its steepest evening climb. Heat buys climbs (r = —), but colour tells the deeper story: the deep-belly days sit exactly where the climbs are steepest. A duck that dives deepest climbs hardest (dip vs climb, r = —).

Drag horizontally to filter days by temperature — the heatmap, stats and table follow · colour = that day’s deepest dip (same scale as the heatmap) · dashed curve = mean climb per 2° bin

In the comfort band — roughly 17–23°C — almost nobody heats or cools, and on cloudless days the net load dives deepest. But what the sun gives at noon it takes at dusk: the deepest dives belong to the steepest climbs, because the belly is exactly the hole the sunset has to fill. The far right is summer — air conditioning racing the sunset. The gentle evenings are the dark ones — overcast, unremarkable days when solar never dug the hole in the first place. The duck, in other words, is carved by clear skies: the clearer the day, the deeper the dive and the harder the climb.

Method, honestly

  • The dataEverything here is synthetic, generated in your browser from seed 1123581321: fifteen years (2010–2024) at hourly resolution, at 15-minute resolution for 2024 — — observations per series. No real utility was harmed, consulted, or leaked.
  • The physicsSolar follows a real sun-position model (declination, hour angle, latitude 37.6°N) with clear-sky falloff, temperature derating, snow days, and an AR(1) cloud system with synoptic persistence. Temperature is a seasonal sinusoid plus diurnal cycle plus OU weather anomalies — with scripted events (the February freeze, the July heat wave, the April deep-duck weekend).
  • The demandHour-of-day shapes for weekdays and weekends, temperature response with a comfort band, holiday factors, winter-lighting term, hourly AR(1) noise — and, from 2018, an EV charging profile that peaks at 20:00. Net load = demand − solar, computed, never invented.
  • The numbersEvery figure quoted in the text — crossover year, rooftop hours, steepest climbs, the correlation coefficient — is computed from this dataset at load time, not hard-coded. Solar share of 2024 energy: —. Installed capacity grows — over the window.
  • The craftAll charts are hand-drawn on canvas at device-pixel resolution; one shared animation loop; delta-time animations; resize via ResizeObserver; pausable on tab-hide; keyboard-operable charts; a screen-reader live region for selections; sonified heatmap hover (toggle in the masthead); respects prefers-reduced-motion.
  • DisclosureMeridian is a fictional mid-latitude coastal city of 2.1 million. The story is real physics on invented data — a demonstration of a shape, not a forecast.
