01 — THE FIFTY-MINUTE SLIP

Every day, the ocean is late.

On 1 February, the first high water slides past Merrow Point at 03:12. The next day it arrives at 04:03, then 04:54. Day after day the sea turns up roughly fifty minutes later, as if it kept time on a clock whose day lasts twenty-four hours and fifty minutes.

Such a clock exists. It is the Moon. Sweeping round the Earth once every 24 h 50 min, it drags its bulge of water past the coast twice per circuit — once beneath it, once on the far side of the world. Solar noon never moves. The Moon's hour does.

02 — THE SPIRAL MONTH

One month winds the spiral once.

Plot the hour of every high water as an angle on a 24-hour dial, one tide further out from the centre, and the record becomes a spiral. In the course of a lunar month the hour of high water makes a full tour of the clock — dawn tides become midnight tides become evening tides — then the pattern closes on itself and begins again.

The silver hand marks the hour the Moon passes overhead; each high-water dot clings to it, always a little behind. Brush the timeline at the foot of the chart to wind more days onto the spiral.

03 — SPRINGS AND NEAPS

Twice a month, the sea takes a deep breath.

No single tide repeats exactly. Step back, and the record breathes: a fortnight of ambitious tides — springs — then a fortnight of reluctant ones — neaps. Springs arrive when Sun and Moon pull along the same line, at new moon and full moon, every 14¾ days. Neaps follow a week later, when the pulls cross and partly cancel.

The ribbon along the top is the Moon itself, drawn from the same arithmetic that moves the water. Find a full moon: the tallest water sits a day or so beneath it.

04 — THE MOON'S GRIP

Ask the Moon directly.

Each dot is one day of the record: where it falls on the Moon's calendar — its age, new moon to new moon — and how tall its tides stood. The pattern is not a line but a lobe, twice over. Range peaks just after the syzygies — the age of the tide — and sags in between.

The tallest day in two years — the king tide — is no accident: it fell just after a new moon, with the Moon near the close end of its orbit. The record's tallest springs all cluster near those close passes. Switch the axis to illumination and the two lobes fold into one.

05 — THE HARBOUR

What the Moon costs the harbour.

The ferry Vellamo draws 1.6 m. The bar at the harbour mouth sounds 0.9 m below chart datum, and company rules demand 0.35 m of under-keel water — so the gauge must read 1.05 m or better to sail. On an ordinary day the sea grants two generous windows. Around a spring low it shuts for hours. The port reschedules itself twice a day, every day, to the Moon's arithmetic.

GENERATION PROTOCOL

How this record is made

Water level = mean sea level + six astronomical constituents + seasonal breathing + seeded storm surges + a wisp of correlated noise. Nothing else. The Moon's phase, age and distance come from the same clock, so every relationship you have seen is structural, not styled.

All arithmetic runs on your machine. The page makes no network requests beyond the two typefaces, and degrades gracefully without them. Levels are metres above chart datum (m CD); times are station time (UTC). This dataset is synthetic by construction and must not be used for navigation, obviously.

ABOUT THE DATA

Merrow Point is fictional. Its tide is the real arithmetic of real tide tables — six harmonic constituents (M2, S2, N2, K1, O1, M4) — plus a seeded storm season and noise, generated in your browser from a single integer seed. Springs lag the syzygies by about a day, as they do on many real coasts: the age of the tide.
