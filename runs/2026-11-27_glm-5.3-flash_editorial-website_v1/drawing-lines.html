Borders

Drawing Lines on Water

The sea does not read treaties. A short tour of the world's most argumentative lines: the Arctic rock two countries spent half a century celebrating with whisky, the coastline that grows the harder you measure it, and the legal fiction that borders can be pinned to water.

  • By Sigrid Halvorsen
  • 9 min read
  • First published 24 November 2026
  • 80°29′N 66°06′W

The border between Canada and Denmark runs for thousands of kilometres — through open water, across ice, down the middle of a strait named after a whaler. For most of its length it is invisible, agreed and untroubled. Then, at 80°29′N, it arrives at an island about the size of a city park, and for half a century it stopped.

The rock with two flags

Hans Island — Tartupaluk in Greenlandic, Hans Ø in Danish — sits in the middle of the Nares Strait between Greenland and Ellesmere Island.4 When the two countries drew their maritime boundary in 1973, the line ran through the strait the way a good median line should, and stopped at the island: the negotiators could not agree whether it belonged to one side, the other, or to nobody in particular, and so they left it out — a hole in the middle of a treaty. Both governments were entirely civilised about the hole. Forces from each side visited the island periodically over the following decades, raising flags, swapping the occasional bottle — schnapps from one side, Canadian rye from the other — and leaving notes that said, in effect, welcome to our island. The press called it the whisky war. It ended in 2022, when the two countries agreed to split the island along a natural cleft and let the border continue on its way — producing a land border about a kilometre long and one of the longest settled maritime boundaries on Earth. Fifty years, all told, over a rock nobody lived on; it took that long mostly because neither side was in any hurry to insult the other.

Marginalia · N.1

Tartupaluk — the Greenlandic name — is usually said to refer to the island's kidney-like shape. It is 1.3 square kilometres of rock, snow and wind, with no resources worth the adjective.

The politest territorial dispute in modern history lasted fifty years, and its principal weapons were bottles.

— From the text

Where the line begins

Every boundary at sea has to start somewhere, and international law has settled on a slightly absurd answer: it starts at the edge of the water. The starting point is the low-water line — the coastline as it stands when the tide is out — as marked on large-scale charts officially recognised by the coastal state.1 From that line, the territorial sea extends twelve nautical miles. This works beautifully until you notice what a coastline is: a negotiation between land and water that is renegotiated twice a day. The tide redraws the baseline every few hours. A storm can redraw it permanently. The law expects the low-water line to move; the treaties that cite it freeze it in place; and between the two sits the quiet engineering problem of modern boundary-making — how to draw a permanent line from a moving zero.

Marginalia · N.2

UNCLOS Article 5, the “normal baseline”: “the low-water line along the coast as marked on large-scale charts officially recognized by the coastal State.” The map, again, is doing the legal work.

The coastline that fights back

There is a second, stranger problem, noticed by the physicist Lewis Fry Richardson while he was measuring frontiers for a study of why states fight: the length of a border depends on the length of your ruler. Use a long one and you skip the small wiggles; use a short one and you catch every cove, and the total grows. Richardson found the growth obeyed a tidy rule, and Benoît Mandelbrot — who took the observation and built a mathematics around it — made it famous with a 1967 paper asking how long the coast of Britain is.2 The answer is: longer, always, than the last time you measured. Reference works still disagree about the length of Portugal's coast by more than a hundred per cent, and both are, in some sense, correct.3 A coastline is not a fact. It is a function — of scale, of patience, of nerve.

Ask a coastline its length, and it will ask which ruler you brought.

— From the text

Future tense

None of this is an argument against drawing lines; the alternatives are worse. Lines at sea are agreements to behave. They allocate fish, cables, shipping lanes, oil, rescue obligations and, occasionally, grief. But they should be drawn knowing what they are pinned to. The baseline moves; the paper does not. The rock splits, the whisky flows, and the tide comes in twice a day, as it did before any of it, indifferent to jurisprudence.

Every border is a sentence in the future tense, written by people who will not be there when the grammar is tested. The best that can be said of the good ones — the cleft on Tartupaluk, the median down Nares Strait — is that they were drawn slowly, argued rarely, and are true for now.
