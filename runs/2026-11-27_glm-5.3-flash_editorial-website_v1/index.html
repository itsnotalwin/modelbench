Issue 07 — Uncertain Ground

COVER — The Coral Sea, sheet 07-A. The dashed outline is Sandy Island, carried on charts from 1876 to 2012; both ship tracks are drawn after contemporaneous reports. The depth sounding in red — 1,401 metres — is what the R/V Southern Surveyor found where the island should have been.

In this issue

  1. 01

    Feature·Phantom Geography·10 min

    The Island That Refused to Exist

    For 136 years, Sandy Island sat neatly between Australia and New Caledonia — charted, named, carried from atlas to atlas, though nobody had ever stood on it. In November 2012, a research vessel steamed to its coordinates and found open water, fourteen hundred metres down.

    By Mara Whitford · First published 12 November 2026

  2. 02

    Essay·Terra Incognita·8 min

    In Praise of the Blank Spot

    The blank spots are going extinct. A defence of the last places we have chosen not to know precisely — the deepest water, the highest unclimbed peak, the emptiest point in the ocean — and an argument that some ignorance is worth keeping.

    By Tomás Rebelo · First published 18 November 2026

  3. 03

    Feature·Borders·9 min

    Drawing Lines on Water

    The sea does not read treaties. A short tour of the world's most argumentative lines: the Arctic rock two countries spent half a century celebrating with whisky, the coastline that grows the harder you measure it, and the legal fiction that borders can be pinned to water.

    By Sigrid Halvorsen · First published 24 November 2026

From the editor

Every map is an argument, and the argument is never finished. We chose the theme of this issue — uncertain ground — because the map has never been more confident, or less innocent. Satellites pass over the whole Earth every day; the device in your pocket locates you to within a few metres; the blank patches of the nineteenth century have been filled, coloured and priced. And yet the ground itself refuses to cooperate. Islands prove to have been paperwork all along. Coastlines move faster than the treaties that pin them. Whole mountains remain unmapped not because we cannot reach them, but because we have decided, for reasons we find it hard to state, not to go.

The three pieces in this issue trace that friction from different directions. Mara Whitford follows the most polite deletion in the history of geography: an island that spent 136 years on charts and then stopped existing. Tomás Rebelo defends the blank spot as a form of humility, from the emptiest point in the ocean to a mountain Bhutan has chosen to leave alone. Sigrid Halvorsen spends time with the people who draw borders on water — a job like carpentry, but for a material that will not hold still.

Uncertainty, we have come to think, is not the map's failure. It is the map's most honest feature, and the one we work hardest to hide.

Teresa AlmeidaEditor, Meridian

Notes from the archive

  • 12 November 1876

    The barque Velocity, whaling out of Sydney, logs “Sandy I.” in the Coral Sea. The name will be copied from chart to chart for 136 years, through the age of steam, the age of satellites and the age of the pocket atlas, until somebody finally goes to look.

  • May 1967

    Benoît Mandelbrot asks “How Long Is the Coast of Britain?” in the journal Science. The answer, he demonstrates, depends on the length of your ruler — and grows without limit as the ruler shrinks. Two reference works still disagree about the length of Portugal's coast by more than a hundred per cent.

  • In progress

    The Seabed 2030 project reports that barely a quarter of the ocean floor has been mapped to modern standards. The target — a complete, freely available chart of every ocean — falls due in 2030. Most of Earth, in other words, is still drawn from echoes.
