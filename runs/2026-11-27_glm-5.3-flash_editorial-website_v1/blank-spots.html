Terra Incognita

In Praise of the Blank Spot

The blank spots are going extinct. A defence of the last places we have chosen not to know precisely — the deepest water, the highest unclimbed peak, the emptiest point in the ocean — and an argument that some ignorance is worth keeping.

  • By Tomás Rebelo
  • 8 min read
  • First published 18 November 2026
  • 48°52′S 123°24′W

There is a globe in New York, cast around 1510, carrying a sentence that has outlived every empire that ignored it. Near the eastern edge of Asia, in the space where the mapmaker ran out of verified world, two words are inscribed: HC SVNT DRACONES. Here are dragons.1 The phrase is usually quoted as a joke about medieval gullibility, but the joke misses what the sentence is doing. It is not claiming that dragons exist. It is admitting, in public, in Latin, exactly where the knowledge ends — and dressing the admission, because this is a Renaissance object after all, in a small flourish of fright.

Where the dragons went

The dragons are gone now, of course. What replaced them is more confident and less truthful about itself: a continuous surface with no visible edges, where nothing is ever missing — only unrendered. The blank spot was the old map's grammar for “I do not know.” The modern map has no such grammar. It draws everything at once, and where it has nothing to draw, it shows you water: the default texture of not-knowing is a pleasant blue.

Alfred Korzybski's old warning — the map is not the territory — was aimed at the confusion between a representation and the world.2 Our era has added a subtler confusion: between completeness and correctness. A map that leaves a gap is obviously unfinished. A map that fills every gap looks finished, whether or not anything underneath was actually checked. On a screen, confidence is a colour value.

Marginalia · N.1

The globe in question is the Hunt–Lenox globe, held by the New York Public Library. It is about eleven centimetres across. The dragons sit near Southeast Asia — then the edge of the known, now the centre of the printed world.

A blank on the map was an invitation. A blank in the database is a policy.

— From the text

The emptiest point on Earth

Consider the most deliberately empty place we have. In the South Pacific, at 48°52.6′S, 123°23.6′W, sits Point Nemo: the oceanic pole of inaccessibility, the point farthest from any land. Its nearest shores are three scattered dots — Ducie Island, Motu Nui beside Easter Island, and Maher Island off Antarctica — each roughly 2,688 kilometres away. Nothing about Point Nemo was discovered; it was computed. It is an absence with coordinates, and the absence turns out to be useful. When space agencies retire large spacecraft — the Mir station among them, in 2001 — they aim the debris at this stretch of water, where weak currents, sparse biology and long distance from shipping make it the least likely place on Earth for falling metal to meet anything that matters. Well over two hundred vehicles have now been parked there: a cemetery with no headstones.3

Marginalia · N.2

The South Pacific gyre is among the least biologically productive stretches of open ocean on the planet — one of the reasons agencies chose it. Beneath the cemetery: kilometres of soft, dark sediment.

The place is named after Captain Nemo, Verne's submariner — “no one” in Latin, the man who left every flag behind. The name is more accurate than the agencies intended. It is the one patch of the planet where nobody is, on purpose.

The mountain no one may climb

Absence can also be a decision. Gangkhar Puensum, on the border of Bhutan and Tibet, is generally reckoned the highest unclimbed mountain on Earth at 7,570 metres — not because it is technically out of reach, but because Bhutan, which regards its high peaks as the abodes of spirits, prohibited mountaineering on them in the 1990s and later made the ban effectively total. Other summits of comparable height were conquered decades ago; this one remains unclimbed the way a letter remains unopened. Its blank spot is not ignorance — every contour is known. It is restraint, formalised.

Marginalia · N.3

The name is usually translated as “white peak of the three spiritual brothers.” Expeditions in the 1980s and 1990s turned back on its flanks; then the law closed the door.

The last blank spots will not be discovered. They will be defended.

— From the text

Against completeness

It is fashionable to say that the age of exploration is over and the map is done. Barely a quarter of the sea floor has been mapped to modern standards; a complete chart of the world ocean remains a project, not a fact. But the deeper point is not that gaps remain. It is that we should keep some on purpose. A map that admits its limits teaches the reader the shape of their own ignorance. A map that claims totality teaches only trust. The dragons were never the point; the point was the honesty of the sentence around them.

So here is a proposal for the cartographers of the next century, offered by an amateur with no authority and less shame: somewhere on every general-purpose map, leave a blank. Label it, if you must, in the old grammar — not HC SVNT DRACONES, but something truer: WE HAVE NOT BEEN. Three words, set in small capitals, doing more for the reader's sense of the world than any amount of rendered detail. The blank spot is not the map's failure to grow up. It is the last place where the map admits it had a childhood.
