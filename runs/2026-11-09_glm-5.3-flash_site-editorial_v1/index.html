AN ATLAS OF THE CHAPPE OPTICAL TELEGRAPH · 1791–1855

Lines of Sight

For sixty years, before electricity learned to speak, France talked to itself at the speed of daylight — a lattice of towers flashing words across 5,000 kilometres of sky. This is how the machine worked, what it carried, who robbed it, and why it died.

PLATE Nº IBEFORE 1791

The Tyranny of Distance

The Revolution had made France a democracy in a hurry. Nothing in the country's geography had agreed to keep up.

In the spring of 1793 the French Republic was fighting most of Europe, and it learned the news the way the Romans had — by horse, at the speed of a horse, on roads that had not materially improved since the Middle Ages. A dispatch from Strasbourg, on the Rhine frontier, took four days to reach Paris. The decisions of the National Convention were already two days old when they left the hall; the answers were older still.

It is hard to overstate how much this shaped politics. A government that governs at the pace of its slowest courier governs, in effect, the past. Armies at the frontier moved faster than the orders chasing them. Markets in Lyon traded on rumours days stale. And every crisis — a mutiny, a siege, a landing in Brittany — began its second life as a rumour before it arrived as a fact, because the fact was four days walking.

"The Republic was negotiating with yesterday."— the problem, stated plainly

Engineers understood the arithmetic of the problem exactly. A courier on the post roads — changing horses at the relays that stitched the kingdom together — could average perhaps ten kilometres an hour over a long day, and no arrangement of fresh horses could beat the horizon. Light itself travelled the distance in a fraction of a second; the trick was to find something that could carry a message on light, and be read a hundred kilometres away.

Smoke had signalled since antiquity, and coastwise navies had long used flags. What nobody had yet built was a language for distance: a code compact enough to say real things, carried on an apparatus sturdy enough to stand on a hilltop in a wind, and legible enough to be read through a telescope by a stranger with cold hands. Three brothers from a village near Le Mans were about to try all three problems at once.

NOTEThe four-days figure for Strasbourg-to-Paris news and its reduction to two hours under the telegraph is the standard comparison recorded in the Wikipedia article on the Chappe telegraph, and it anchors every speed claim in this atlas. Before the towers, "fast" news was still road news.
PLATE Nº II1791–1805

Brothers of the Beam

Claude Chappe tried to build a telegraph out of clocks, out of sound, and out of electricity. What worked was a pair of wooden arms.

Claude Chappe was born in 1763 in Brûlon, a village in the Sarthe, and trained for the church before physics got him. When the Revolution closed his career prospects, it opened his laboratory: he and his brothers — Ignace, Pierre-François and René — began experimenting with ways to send messages faster than a courier.

Their first machine was a cuckoo clock of a thing: stations equipped with synchronised pendulums that ticked in unison, so that a signal sent from one end arrived with its position on the dial intact. Claude tried to carry the synchronising impulse by sound, by smoke, and seriously by electricity — the problem was insulation, which the eighteenth century had not yet invented; a wet rope ruined the circuit. The pendulum machine worked at country distances and failed as an idea: every station had to agree on time before it could agree on a word.

On 2–3 March 1791, between Brûlon and Parcé-sur-Sarthe, the brothers demonstrated the line for their patrons with the message "L'Assemblée nationale récompensera les expériences utiles au public" — the National Assembly will reward experiments useful to the public. It was flattery aimed at the budget committee, and it worked.

§ 2.1The Republic buys a nervous system

In 1792 Chappe proposed a line to the Legislative Assembly, and the machine acquired its name around the same time: télégraphe, the far-writer. In April 1793 the deputy Gillet-Romme reported favourably to the Convention; on 25 July Chappe was appointed by decree; on 4 August 1793 the Paris–Lille line was approved. The Republic, at war, had ordered a nervous system.

The line ran 230 kilometres from Paris to the northern front through fifteen towers, and it was working by August 1794. On 15 August the wire carried word of the recapture of Le Quesnoy. Two weeks later, on 30 August, it carried the surrender of Condé-sur-l'Escaut — a dispatch that reached the Convention while the battle's smoke was still settling on the horizon, in minutes, ending with the flourish "Condé être restitué à République, reddition ce matin 6 heures." Deputies who had waited days for frontier news now heard it within the hour. The machine had passed its audition, and for the next half-century it would carry the state.

VIGNETTE(Invented, in period idiom — the record keeps the dispatches, not the voices.) A deputy is said to have asked how the thing could be believed. The answer, in the spirit of 1794: the towers lie no more than the couriers do, and they lie more slowly.

§ 2.2The inventor, at the bottom of a well

Claude Chappe did not profit calmly. Rivals claimed the idea was not his; accusations of plagiarism chased him for years, and the constant litigation over priority wore him down alongside recurring illness. On 23 January 1805, at the telegraph administration in Paris, he threw himself down the machinery well and died. He was forty-one. The network he invented went on speaking without him — 534 towers at its peak, five thousand kilometres of sky — and the street that carries his name today, rue Chappe below Montmartre, is nowhere near a tower. The machines, famously, had stood on high ground.

NOTEAccounts of Chappe's final months — chronic illness, despondency over the priority disputes — are summarised in the Wikipedia biographies and the IEEE/ETHW profile cited in Sources. The location of the well: the telegraph administration's Paris offices.
PLATE Nº III1794–1855

The Atlas

One line became a star, the star a lattice — and for a decade the lattice wore an Empire. Scrub through sixty-four years of growth.

The Paris–Lille line proved the machine; the rest of the century spread it. Strasbourg followed in 1797, Brest in 1799, then Lyon, the Alps, Italy, and the whole south-west. Between 1803 and 1814 the network followed the borders of annexed France — Brussels, Antwerp, Amsterdam, Mainz, Turin, Milan, Venice — and retreated with them. By 1844 the atlas peaks: 534 towers, more than 5,000 kilometres of line, twenty-nine cities linked, with optical cousins as far as Sweden, where Abraham Niclas Edelcrantz had built his own in 1794, and Britain, whose Admiralty shutters watched the Channel coast.

Read the map as a political instrument. The first lines connect Paris to armies; the middle lines connect Paris to fleets and to the Empire's annexes; the late lines — the 1830s and 1840s laterals — thicken the web for administration and commerce. Growth follows power, not geography.

METHODRoutes are drawn schematically through principal towns; line lengths are modelled on this projection and tower counts at roughly one per 12 km, except where documented figures exist (Paris–Lille: 230 km, 15 towers; Paris–Brest: 58 stations). The 1844 peak — 534 towers, 5,000 km, 29 cities — is the documented figure.
PLATE Nº IVTHE STATION

Anatomy of a Tower

A sky-blue mast on a hilltop, two black arms, a miniature copy in a hut, and a person with cold hands and two telescopes. Eight parts, numbered on the plate.

A Chappe station is a study in delegation. The operator never touches the great arms: he poses a small working replica — the — and clockwork geared to counterweights repeats his gestures six metres overhead, where the and its two stand against the sky. The mast itself was painted sky blue so that it would vanish against the horizon and the black arms would seem to float — a machine designed to be seen without appearing to exist.

Reading was harder than writing. Through telescopes of 30× to 65× fixed on each neighbour, an operator watched the previous tower's pose, decided which of ninety-eight positions it showed, and copied it forward. Towers stood 5 to 15 km apart on whatever high ground the line of sight allowed — hence the species of hilltop ruins that still punctuates the French countryside. Two men worked each station at first — one reading, one writing — later often one, on construction-worker wages, every day the sky was usable. Inspectors rode circuits of about ten towers. None of the tower men held a ; they relayed a language they could not read.

The Argenteuil man signs №64 like a drunk signs his name — every wing one degree drunker. Counted eleven crows on the regulator at noon. Wind from the west all day; the louvres sang."
PLATE Nº VTHE CODE

A Grammar of Arms

Ninety-eight poses, six of them housekeeping. Numbers, not letters. A dictionary of pages. And a throughput you can feel in your patience.

The final design is almost austere. A with two positions; two with seven each: 2 × 7 × 7 = 98 poses, of which six were reserved as — repetition, waiting, absence, and the that erases the previous symbol. The remaining 92 carried all of France's traffic, and they carried it as numbers, not letters.

Text travelled in pairs. The first signal named a page of the ; the second named a line on it: 92 × 92 = 8,464 possible entries, so that two poses could summon a word, a name, or a whole ready-made phrase. The 1794 book listed 846 words and phrases; supplementary volumes followed until the dictionary ran to many thousands of entries. One documented example, beloved of this subject: page 50, position 87 — "ignorance."

Speed was the tax. A good station set a symbol every twenty to thirty seconds — two or three a minute — and the whole relay paid the toll. A short dispatch crossed the 230 km to Lille in under nine minutes; in 1838 a Paris–Toulouse dispatch took two and a half hours. Roughly 0.4 words per minute end to end: slow as handwriting, incomparable to any road. The towers did not beat horses at writing. They beat them at arriving.

NOTEThe pose arithmetic (2 × 7 × 7), the six service signals, the page-and-line code (92 × 92; "ignorance" at page 50, position 87), and the throughput figures (2–3 symbols/minute; Paris–Lille under nine minutes; Paris–Toulouse 2.5 hours in 1838) are from the Wikipedia articles on the Chappe telegraph and optical telegraphy and the sources listed on the last plate.

§ 5.1Write to Lille

Now send something. The encoder spells letter by letter — a teaching approximation this atlas makes honestly: the historical system preferred whole words and phrases, precisely because a at two symbols a minute could not afford the alphabet. Watch the relay: each symbol must be read, decoded, and re-set at every tower down the line before it may move again. Distance is paid for, station by station.

PLATE Nº VI1834–1837

The Bordeaux Fraud

Two bankers, two bribed operators, 121 parcels of haberdashery, and one stolen channel hidden inside the state's own grammar. The first data-network crime, reconstructed.

In 1834 the Paris set the price of — government bonds, the era's great traded security — and the price moved with political news. Traders in Bordeaux saw that price when it arrived by mail: days later, at the speed of the diligence, after the market had closed and the margin had evaporated. The telegraph could have told them in hours. But the wire belonged to the state, carried only official in code, and admitted nobody's portfolio.

François and Joseph Blanc, bond traders of Bordeaux (François would later found the casino at Monte Carlo), solved the problem by inventing a channel inside the message itself. They bribed two employees of the Tours section of the Paris–Bordeaux line — Guibout and Renaud, paid 1,500 francs to join and then 150 francs a month plus 20 francs a message, against a normal wage of about 1.50 francs a day — to corrupt the relay, exactly the grammar you saw in the last chapter. Into the stream of official poses the Tours operator inserted one spurious symbol carrying the previous day's market movement, immediately followed by the code. The annulment ordered every downstream station to pretend the symbol had never happened: the message arrived at Bordeaux perfect and official. But the inserted pose had already been shown — flashed briefly between towers — and a former telegraph employee posted outside Bordeaux with a telescope read it as it passed.

What to watch, and when, was signalled from Paris in parcels. Gloves, socks or neckties named the direction of the market; their colour graded its size. Over the scheme's life some 121 such parcels crossed from Paris to Tours. The operation ran for roughly two years — the first recorded man-in-the-middle attack on a data network, executed with wooden arms and daylight.

SOURCESThe Blanc affair is documented through Tom Standage's account in The Victorian Internet (1998) and the summaries of it on Bruce Schneier's blog, The Economist's 1843, and Amusing Planet — all cited on the last plate. Details of payment, parcels and exposure follow those accounts; two of them differ on precisely who betrayed the sick operator, so this atlas states only what they agree on.

§ 6.2The verdict, and the law that followed it

The scheme unravelled in 1836, when the corrupted Tours operator fell ill and the arrangement came to light. The Blancs were tried — and walked free. No statute covered what they had done: they had not tampered with any message the state received, and "misusing a data network" was not yet a category of crime. The law was rewritten in 1837 to make it one; the Vocabulary of every network since has carried the echo. It is worth noticing what the fraud actually exploited: not the machinery, not the code, but a role — the trusted relay that everyone downstream assumes is honest. The towers were never hacked. The morning shift was.

PLATE Nº VII1845–1855

Fade-out

The new machine learned the old language first. Then it buried it — politely, tower by tower, in a single decade.

Electricity arrived in France as a translator. The first electric telegraph line — Paris to Rouen, working in 1845 — used the Foy– dial, whose two needles deliberately reproduced the angles of Chappe's arms. A generation trained on towers could read the new machine at a glance; the revolution disguised itself as an upgrade. On 3 July 1846 a law ordered an electric Paris–Lille, and the comparison was never close again: electricity ignored clouds, ignored dusk, ignored the 20-second toll of daylight, and worked at night.

The towers shut down in the order they had been built. The historic Paris–Lille line — the Republic's first wire — closed to electricity in 1847; the mainland network was gone by 1854–55, when France's last optical line was abandoned and the last prefecture, Mende, joined the electric net. The future, characteristically, had already emigrated: Chappe-style coastal lines opened around Tunis in 1848, Algiers–Oran and Algiers–Constantine in 1853, and a military optical line ran between Varna and Balchik in 1854–56 — the newest towers on Earth being built while the oldest network was being unbolted. Watch them go grey in the atlas: scrub to 1855 and read what remains.

What survived is everywhere. The towers' stone went back to farms; some hilltop platforms became signal or survey points; the word telegraph changed employers overnight. And the machine's deeper legacy passed into language itself: — the name of the movable-arm signal — now names a programming primitive that keeps threads from colliding. Every computer that runs several things at once still consults a little piece of the Chappe grammar, though the arms have become integers and the sky has become silicon.

So end the sixty-four years with the honest ledger. The optical telegraph was slow, deaf to night and weather, and gloriously easy to compromise — and it was still the fastest thing its century had, the first network a modern state ever operated at continental scale, and the proof of a principle that has outlived every machine built on it since: that distance is an engineering problem, not a fate. Everything after it — the electric wire, the cable, the radio, the fibre under the sea — is a series of answers to the question the five brothers from Brûlon asked on a hillside in 1791.

PLATE Nº IXVOCABULARY

The Keeper's Vocabulary

Sixteen terms this atlas leans on. Every dotted term in the text opens its entry here.

semaphore
From Greek sēma (sign) + phoros (bearing): a movable-arm signal. Coined for machines like Chappe's; now also the name of a concurrency primitive in software — the old word still runs inside your computer.
regulator
The great central beam of a Chappe mast, 4.60 × 0.35 m. It took two working positions — horizontal or vertical — and carried the two indicators at its ends.
indicator
Either of the two smaller wings (2.00 × 0.30 m) mounted at the ends of the regulator. Each could take seven distinct positions, giving 2 × 7 × 7 = 98 poses.
manipulator
The miniature working replica of the mast at the operator's station, geared to the real arms. The operator posed the small copy; the large one obeyed.
code book
The printed dictionary pairing numbers with words and phrases. Superintendents held it; ordinary operators relayed poses they could not read.
service code
One of six reserved poses used for housekeeping rather than text — repetition, waiting, operator absence, and the annulment that cancels the previous symbol.
annulment
The service code meaning "ignore the previous symbol". Officially a correction; in the Blanc fraud, a hiding place.
dispatch
A formal state message in transit: a sequence of code numbers, relayed pose by pose between towers.
relay
One tower in a chain. Each relay copied what it saw to the next, so a dispatch travelled at the speed of daylight plus human reaction.
line of sight
The unobstructed visual path between two towers. Lines were engineered around hills — the reason Chappe stations perch on highpoints 5–15 km apart.
rentes
French government bonds, the era's most traded security. Their price moved with political news — which is exactly why the Blancs wanted it early.
diligence
The scheduled mail coach. Paris-to-Bordeaux market news travelled by diligence for days; the telegraph took hours — but carried none of it, officially.
Foy–Breguet
The electric telegraph of 1845 whose two needles deliberately reproduced Chappe's arm angles, so a generation trained on towers could read the new machine at a glance.
shutter telegraph
Rival optical designs of the 1790s — Edelcrantz's shutters in Sweden, Murray's shutters for the British Admiralty — using hinged panels rather than pivoted beams.
bourse
The stock exchange. The Paris Bourse set the price of rentes; Bordeaux traders saw it late, which is the whole story of Chapter VI.
ingénieur-télégraphe
Chappe's title under the 1793 decree: engineer of the telegraph, with the network and its builders under his command.
