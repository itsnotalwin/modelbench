№ 00 — Preliminary

Read me first

00

Grey is not the absence of colour. It is the presence of judgement. Every grey on this page is a measured quantity of light — one number between 0 and 255 — weighed, named and set down on purpose.

This is a field guide in the old, honest sense: a closed world, catalogued. Two hundred and fifty-six specimens live along the single narrow road that runs from absolute black to absolute white, and none of them strays. Nothing here is coloured — not the type, not the rules, not one shadow — because the subject forbids it, and so does the editor.

Ahead: a complete census of the ramp (Chapter I), the photographer’s tonal scale (II), machines that fake grey out of black and white dots (III), a bench for measuring legibility (IV), and a short natural history of the honest tone (V).

“Between black and white lie two hundred and fifty-five honest disagreements.”

— the editor, in the introduction, meaning it

Chapter II — The photographer’s scale

The zones

II

Photographers sorted the printable world into eleven values, Zone 0 to Zone X, and learned to place their shadows deliberately instead of hoping. The strip maps every zone to its grey; press one to read its description.

V mapped value 118 · #767676

Middle grey

The zones are print-relative, defined by Ansel Adams and Fred Archer around 1940. The swatches map CIE L* 0–100 into sRGB — an honest approximation of values that live in a denser tonal space.

Chapter III — The machines

The dither laboratory

III

Ink can do two tones; photographs need a hundred. Presses, fax machines and the 512 × 342 screen of 1984 faked everything in between with patterns of pure black and pure white. The laboratory runs the four classic tricks, live, on plates this page draws itself.

Subject
Method
Tones
4 tones

The Bayer 4 × 4 threshold matrix: every pixel on the plate compares itself against one of these sixteen values (scaled 0–1), tiled across the image like a weave.

Threshold rounds each pixel to the nearest available tone: fast, honest, and it eats detail into bands.

Ordered dithering (Bayer) adds a fixed woven threshold pattern — detail survives, at the price of visible crosshatch.

Error diffusion (Floyd–Steinberg, 1976) pushes each pixel’s rounding mistake into its neighbours until the pattern dissolves into grain. Atkinson spreads only three-quarters of the error, keeping highlights luminous — the house style of the early Macintosh screen.

Chapter IV — The arithmetic of legibility

The contrast bench

IV

Grey text on grey paper is a decision with arithmetic attached. Slide the ink and the paper; the bench computes the WCAG 2 contrast ratio, badges it, and sets the verdict in front of you.

17.40 : 1

    ratio = (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), with relative luminance on the sRGB curve. AA body text needs 4.5 : 1; AAA needs 7 : 1; large text and UI need 3 : 1.

    Chapter V — The record

    A natural history of grey

    V

    Ten exhibits from a very long file: humanity has been faking, fixing and loving the honest tone for far longer than it has had screens.

    1. c. 32,000 BCE

      Charcoal

      At Chauvet, artists grind charred wood and bone into the first grey — whole herds and hunters drawn by firelight, an entire tonal world made of soot.

    2. c. 1300–1500

      Grisaille

      Panel painters underpaint whole scenes in greys — grisaille — modelling pure value before any colour is permitted. Finished grisaille windows trick the eye into reading paint as carved stone.

    3. c. 1450

      The engraved line

      Intaglio engravers put grey inside black ink: pure black lines bitten into copper, tone built entirely from the density of hatching. Dürer turns the hatch into a science.

    4. 1826–27

      Fixing a shadow

      Joseph Nicéphore Niépce exposes a pewter plate from his upstairs window at Le Gras for most of a day. The earliest surviving photograph is, of course, grey.

    5. 1880s

      The halftone dot

      Printers learn to break a photograph into dots — big where dark, small where light — and the eye obligingly blurs them back into tone. Newspapers become pictorial.

    6. 1976

      The error spreads

      Floyd and Steinberg publish a four-neighbour error-diffusion filter. It becomes the standard way to smuggle continuous tone through machines that can only say black or white.

    7. 1984

      One bit

      The Macintosh ships with a 512 × 342, one-bit-per-pixel screen — two tones, no compromise. Its icons and typography prove that a bitmap is a style, not a limitation.

    8. 1989

      Four shades, a pocket

      The Game Boy’s 160 × 144 LCD offers four grey levels. In the framebuffer they are honest greys; the plastic tints them green, and nobody minds.

    9. 1997–2007

      Paper that greys like paper

      E Ink spins out of MIT in 1997; its microcapsules shuffle black and white pigment under an electric field. The Librie (2004) and Kindle (2007) print whole libraries in grey, at a watt-hour a page.

    10. Today

      The deliberate tone

      Every screen you own can show sixteen million colours. Photographers still print in silver gelatin, designers still reach for #333, and the ramp keeps all two hundred and fifty-six of its residents.

    Chapter VI — The making

    Colophon

    VI

    The type

    Set entirely in the reader’s own typefaces — a high-contrast serif for display, a workhorse serif for prose, a monospace for data — because a field guide should look like it was printed somewhere, and a monograph has no business requesting forty weights of anything.

    The machinery

    Three files: one HTML, one stylesheet, one script. No frameworks, no build step, nothing fetched over the network. Every plate is drawn by the page itself — canvas noise, SVG hatching, plain arithmetic — at the moment you arrive.

    The discipline

    Landmarks, a skip link, a keyboard path through every instrument, and honour for prefers-reduced-motion. Press G anywhere for the night edition. The palette is enforced by conviction: black, white, and the two hundred and fifty-four steps between.
