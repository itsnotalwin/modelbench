02

The screen

Ruled glass · 1882

In 1882 a Munich engraver named Georg Meisenbach patented a way to turn a photograph into a printable surface. The problem he solved was one of grammar. A photograph is a continuum of tone; a letterpress plate is a binary instrument. Ink is either there or it is not.

1Meisenbach’s patent covered a process for making copper printing plates from photographs. The cross-line screen that followed is usually credited to Max Levy, working in Philadelphia in the early 1890s. ↩

His answer was to break the picture into a grid and let the size of each mark carry the tone. Where the photograph was bright the mark shrank; where it darkened the mark swelled until its neighbours touched and the paper vanished between them. At reading distance the eye averages the marks back into a smooth gradient. Close up, the illusion collapses into arithmetic: a field of dots, evenly spaced, unevenly fed.

Within a decade the halftone had swallowed the illustrated press. Every newspaper photograph ever printed is a descendant of that grid — as is every laser-printed invoice, every dithered gradient in a photograph app, and, in a sense, this page.

A photograph is continuous. A press is binary. The dot is the argument between them.

Two numbers govern a screen. Pitch is the distance between dot centres; it decides how much detail survives and how coarse the texture feels. Angle is the rotation of the grid against the page: forty-five degrees for a single black plate, because the eye is least sensitive to diagonal structure, and because a grid set square to the type would beat against the letterforms.

Then there is dot gain. Ink does not stop at the edge of a dot: it spreads into the paper, so a dot drawn at forty per cent covers more than forty per cent, and the midtones darken first. Pressmen compensated by etching the plate lighter. Digital screens inherit the same problem in a different costume — a pixel is a square, and squares spread when they are resampled.

A printer calls this a step wedge: five patches of ink, from almost nothing to almost everything. It is the least glamorous object in this essay and the most useful. Every plate on this page is calibrated against something like it.

04

The dither

Two words for grey

The digital screen inherited the problem and changed the rules: a computer can put a dot anywhere. Two families of answer appeared within four years of each other, and they are still the ones we use.

Ordered dithering uses a repeating threshold matrix. In 1973 Bryce Bayer, working at Kodak, published a small recursive table that decides which pixels to light. The result looks like a screen — regular, calm, stubbornly intact after compression, and almost impossible to animate without visible crawling.

Error diffusion does the opposite. In 1976 Robert Floyd and Louis Steinberg described a method that rounds each pixel to black or white and passes the remainder to its neighbours, so the mistake is never lost, only postponed. Bill Atkinson’s variant, written for the first Macintosh in the mid-1980s, deliberately throws most of the error away: highlights stay open, shadows stay clean, and the grain becomes part of the picture.

Ordered or diffused, both answer the same question: how do you say grey with only two words?

05

The angle

Moiré · a beat frequency

Rotate one screen against another and the grids interfere. The pattern you see is not in the ink; it is a beat frequency, produced where two periodic structures are sampled by an eye of fixed resolution. Printers call it moiré and spend their working lives avoiding it.

In four-colour work the plates are separated by thirty degrees so the interference collapses into a rosette too fine to notice. A single black plate has nothing to separate from, so it takes the diagonal — where the eye is least attentive — and trusts the paper to hide the rest.

Colophon

How this sheet was made

Grey Matter is set in the serif and monospaced faces already on your machine. No webfont is downloaded, so the page should weigh less than the picture it describes. Every mark is drawn in the browser: one landscape computed from layered noise, then screened four ways.

There are no image files, no build step, no analytics, and no colour. White, black, and the greys that fall between them.
