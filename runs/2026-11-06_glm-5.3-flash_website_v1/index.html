Manifesto

Two colours, honestly kept

Every plate in this book is printed with a single honest material: ink that is either present or absent. No colour, no anti-aliased compromise — two states, arranged with such patience that the eye gives up counting and starts believing.

Dithering is the name of that arrangement: deliberate noise, added so the eye reads what isn’t there. It was born on the printing press in the 1880s, drafted into computing during the space race, and it survives today in e-readers, thermal receipts and every display that ever faked a grey it could not afford.

This book is its field guide. Seven methods, each with a temperament — some orderly, some restless, one that simply rolls the dice. Read the specimens, compare them line by line, then take the controls in the Plate Room and steer the light yourself.

Noise is the lubricant of perception.

A Brief History

A hundred and forty years of noise

1882

The halftone screen

Georg Meisenbach patents a process for breaking photographs into dots small enough for a printing press. From here on, every “continuous-tone” picture in a newspaper is actually thousands of black dots — dithering, before the word existed.

1940s

Dither, the word

The story goes that engineers fighting sticky gyroscope bearings found that a small, deliberate vibration kept them moving. The trick — noise as lubricant — was filed away, waiting for computers with too few colours.

1973

Bayer’s matrix

At Kodak, Bryce Bayer publishes An optimum method for two-level rendition of continuous-tone pictures. Sixteen thresholds in a crosshatch: ordered dithering as we know it.

1976

Error, passed along

Robert Floyd and Louis Steinberg show that quantisation error can be pushed downhill to a pixel’s neighbours instead of being thrown away. The look of every printer and early web GIF quietly changes.

1984

Grain for everyone

Bill Atkinson’s dithering ships with the Apple Macintosh, and millions of people meet Floyd–Steinberg’s brighter cousin without ever learning its name.

1993

Blue noise

Robert Ulichney’s void-and-cluster algorithm manufactures threshold screens with the evenness of ordered dither and the organic grain of error diffusion — the best of both temperaments.

Now

The two-colour renaissance

E-ink pages, thermal receipts, pixel-art revivals and one-bit design trends keep dithering honest. Two colours remain the cheapest, sharpest and most characterful way to show a shade.

Colophon

How this book was set

Composition

Hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No frameworks, no build step, no external requests — deploy the folder anywhere static and it simply prints.

Type

Set from the system shelf: a serif with bookish manners for reading, a monospace for measurement. If your machine carries finer faces than these, this page quietly borrows them.

Plates

Every image is procedural — spheres, moons, seas and hills computed from noise, then halftoned live at your screen’s resolution. Nothing was photographed; nothing was downloaded.
