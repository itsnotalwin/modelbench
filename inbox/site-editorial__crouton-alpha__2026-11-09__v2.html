Eight kilos of government

The cargo bike is sold as liberation. In this invented census it is better read as a small state: taxed, parked, repaired and counted. What the count shows is not freedom but a new municipal arithmetic.

The bike does not look like government. It looks like a shop with wheels. In Amsterdam the bakfiets — the box bike — carries children, dogs, groceries and the weekly shop, and it does so with a politeness that tempts writers to call it freedom. This article argues the opposite, and argues it with invented numbers that are at least internally honest. The vehicle here is not liberation; it is a municipal instrument that learned to wear a rain cape. The census on which every chart is drawn is fictional: 212 cargo bikes counted at three canals in March, August and November 2024. The counts are invented, the argument is not. A city that counts a vehicle has already governed it, and the interesting question is what the governance feels like at handlebar height.

The method is deliberately plain. A team — two researchers, one counter for each hour — tallied arrivals, cap types, load, and where the bike finally stood. They distinguished the longtail, the front-loader and the bakfiets, because the three do not behave the same. The bakfiets is the subject. It is heavier, slower, more visible, and more likely to be parked badly. Those four adjectives are the municipal problem in miniature. The longtail can hide in a rack; the box bike cannot. The census therefore becomes, without intending to, a study of visibility. What you can see, you count. What you count, you zone. What you zone, you fine.

02

Anatomy of the mask

Anatomy first. The surveyed bakfiets averaged 71 centimetres wide at the box, 2.1 metres long, 8.0 kilograms — no, the census records 8.0 kilograms for the base module, not the whole machine; the whole machine, with batteries and the inevitable child seat, is 24.6 kilograms, and the article’s title keeps the smaller number because that is the number the manufacturer prints. The discrepancy is the story. The box is designed to hold 90 kilograms of groceries and 8 kilograms of platform. The city must house the platform as well as the groceries. A bike lane 1.6 metres wide can accommodate a bakfiets; two bakfietsen meeting in that lane cannot pass. The geometry that sells the bike — volume — is the geometry that defeats the lane.

Note — Masses in this article are invented to make the totals agree: 8.0 kg base + 16.6 kg running gear = 24.6 kg laden-ready. Real manufacturer figures vary; do not cite these as specs.
03

Counting what cannot hide

The counts produce the article’s three numbers, each of which anchors a figure. First, the modal split of cargo trips. Second, the parking search. Third, the noise. The argument that emerges is that the cargo bike is governed less by traffic law than by parking law. The bike is rarely stopped for moving; it is constantly nudged for standing. Of 212 observed arrivals, 74 (35%) parked legally, 81 (38%) parked on the kerb edge, and 57 (27%) parked across a cycle path or against a bridge rail. The figures are invented, but the pattern — visibility determines enforcement — is the real lesson.

The modal split is instructive because it refuses the heroic headline. The cargo bike did not replace the car; it replaced the foot trip with a load. A market run that once walked home with a string bag now rides with a box. The vehicle looks like car replacement and behaves like walking replacement. That is why the city feels ambivalent: the bike is doing a humane job badly for traffic and well for errands, and the counters cannot make those two sentences agree.

Parking search is the second number because it is the one riders feel in the wrists. The invented mean — 11 minutes, peaking at 13 in the late afternoon — is long enough to matter. A car driver tolerates a long search because distance will repay it. A box-bike rider tolerates it for about three days. The census recorded one rider circling the same canal four times before surrendering to a rail. The governance of the cargo bike, in practice, is the design of corners.

04

What, Exactly, Is A Quiet Vehicle?

Noise is the third number, and the most misread. The bakfiets at 20 km/h is quiet enough that pedestrians step into it. Recorded at 2 metres, the invented mean is 54 dB, against 61 dB for a normal bike’s chain and freewheel on cobbles. The cargo bike is quieter than the bike it displaced — and that is its danger. The city regulated noise when noise was the warning system. A silent, wide, child-carrying box removes exactly the sound that made other people pay attention.

The article’s term for the double vision of other riders: looking at the box because it is unmissable, and looking through it because it is silent. Not a real term.

The gaze is the regulatory gap. Pedestrians are hit not because the bike is fast but because it is unreadable. A car announces itself; a normal bike chatters; a box bike arrives as a wardrobe on castors, empty-sounding until the driver rings too late. The article’s claim is not that the bakfiets should be louder by law — that would be absurd — but that a vehicle whose safety depends on being seen must be designed for being seen. The box that hides the rider’s hand signals is the wrong box.

05

The repair economy is the state

Repair is where the state becomes intimate. Of the 212 bikes, 49 had visited a repair stand in the month of the count. The mean wait was 6 days, the mean invoice €148, the mean apology recorded verbatim: “box takes time.” The bike is bought in an afternoon and repaired across a week. That week is the real ownership cost. A car breaks and the owner is inconvenienced; a bakfiets breaks and the household loses school runs. The municipality that counted these bikes did not create the repair gap, but it profits from ignoring it. A city that wants cargo bikes to replace cars must count repair stands the way it counts parking racks.

“A state you can ride is still a state. The bell is quieter, the fine is smaller, the counting is your own.”
06

Verdict: the small state is also a choice

The verdict is not that the bakfiets fails. It is that its success is municipal rather than heroic. It replaces a foot trip, it calms a street, it embarrasses a car. It also needs corners, stands and signals, and it will be governed by those who provide them. Eight kilos of government — the platform, the box, the counted arrival — is not a small burden. It is the burden of any object that asks a dense city to make room without saying so. The honest slogan is not liberation. It is: please widen the corner. The census ends there, because at 19:40 the counters packed up, the canals darkened, and the boxes stood in two uneven rows, waiting for their owners to return and argue, one more time, about where a wardrobe belongs.

Method note

All counts, masses, minutes and decibels in this feature are invented for narrative consistency and do not describe any real Amsterdam census, manufacturer specification or municipal fine. The invented totals are kept arithmetically consistent: 212 = 74 + 81 + 57; modal shares sum to 100%; the mass split sums to 24.6 kg. No real person or operator is described.

§1 The census is invented. Any resemblance to a real count is coincidental. The argument about parking governance does not depend on the exact count.
§2 Noise values are spot-check inventions. Do not use them as safety guidance.
